\section{Analysis -- Total Interaction Cross Section $^{12}$C + $^{12}$C}\label{sec:analysis_cross_sec}
Absolute cross sections of well-known systems such as $^{12}$C + $^{12}$C serve two key purposes. First, they provide an ideal benchmark for testing reaction theories at high precision. Second, they offer an excellent commissioning case for quantifying systematic uncertainties introduced by the experimental setup. Similar measurements have previously been performed at R3B using a different detector configuration with smaller acceptance but reduced background contributions~\cite{ponnath2024measurement,ponnath2023precise}. In contrast, the experiment discussed here employed a significantly larger acceptance but was operated with the detector setup in air, resulting in different background conditions.\\
This section will go through the  analysis step by step from the unpacking stage to the final results of the interaction cross sections. It will start with a short overview of the transmission method used for the cross section measurements. The next step is  the selection of clean incoming $^{12}$C isotopes. Following the identification of the carbon isotopes after the target -- for the measurement of the charge changing cross section and the isotope correction contribution -- and as final step the derivation of total interaction cross sections. \newline
All relevant detector related geometrical and efficiency corrections will be addressed and their influence to the error budget will be discussed.\\

\subsection{Cross Section Measurement via Transmission Method}\label{section:transmission_method}
In its most generic form cross sections give a measure of the probability that a specific reaction will take place when a particle passes a target material~\cite{bethge2007wiedemann}. It is expressed in units of area, with one barn defined as 1 b = 10$^{-24}$ cm$^2$. Cross sections measured in scattering experiments, along with the corresponding energy and angular distributions of the reaction products, provide valuable insight into the underlying interaction dynamics between the projectile and target. Such measurements enable the extraction of fundamental quantities, including coupling constants, form factors, and structure functions.\newline
The total interaction cross section $\sigma_{tot}$ can be derived by looking at the relation between the number of incoming particles (N$_{1}$) and intact particles after the target ($N_{2}$). For an experiment with fixed target with thickness $z$ and volumetric number density $n$ the number of reacted particles in the infinitesimal thin target layer $dz$ can be expressed as~\footnote{This assumption is valid only if the original projectile species cannot be regenerated through secondary reactions. For the $^{12}$C + $^{12}$C system, this condition is fulfilled.}
\begin{equation}
\frac{dN_{2}}{dz} = -n \sigma_{tot} N_{2} \, .
\end{equation}
Solving this differential equation for $N_{2}$ (with the condition $N_{2}$ = $N_{1}$ for $z=0$) discloses an exponential relation
\begin{equation}
N_{2} = N_{1}e^{-n z \sigma}  = N_{1}e^{-N_t\sigma} \, ,
\label{eq:cross_sec}
\end{equation} 
where $n\cdot z$ can be summarized as $N_t$, the total number of scattering centers in the target per unit area. The relation $(N_{2}/N_{1})$, number of intact particles after the target versus number of incoming particles, is often called survival probability $P_{surv}$. For an experimental setup with full geometrical acceptance and 100\% detection efficiency for all surviving carbon ions and no interactions in the setup material the cross section could simply be deduced from Eq.~\ref{eq:cross_sec}. To account for reactions of the projectile that occur within the setup material and first order detector specific distortions of output signals the survival probability $(N_{2}/N_{1})$ has to be divided by the survival probability for an empty target run $P^{E}_{surv}$ = $(N_{2}^E/N_{1}^E)$, where $N_{1}^E$ is the number of incoming particles and $N_{2}^E$ the number of intact particles after the target for an empty run respectively. Thereby the setup specific efficiency($\epsilon_{setup}$) and transmission factor($t_{setup}$) are canceled out to obtain the underlying number of intact particles after the target $\tilde{N_{2}}$:\newline
$N_{2} = \tilde{N_{2}} \cdot t_{setup}\cdot \epsilon_{setup}$\newline
$N_{2}^E = \tilde{N_{2}^E} \cdot \epsilon_{setup}$ with $\frac{\tilde{N_{2}^E}}{N_{1}^E}$ the setup specific transmission factor $t_{setup}$ \newline
\newline
The formula for the cross section $\sigma_{tot}$ for a transmission measurement is
\begin{equation}
	\begin{split}
\sigma_{tot} = -\frac{1}{N_t} ln(\frac{N_{1}^E}{N_{2}^E} \cdot \frac{N_2}{N_1}) = -\frac{1}{N_t} ln(\frac{N_{1}^E}{\tilde{N_{2}^E} \cdot \epsilon_{setup}} \cdot \frac{\tilde{N_{2}} \cdot t_{setup} \cdot \epsilon_{setup}}{N_1}). \\
\text{With $\frac{\tilde{N_{2}^E}}{N_{1}^E} = t_{setup}$} \, ,\\
\sigma_{tot} = -\frac{1}{N_t} ln(\frac{1}{t_{setup}} \cdot \frac{\tilde{N_{2}} \cdot t_{setup}}{N_1}) = -\frac{1}{N_t} ln(\frac{\tilde{N_{2}}}{N_1}) \, .
\label{eq:corr_cross}
	\end{split}
\end{equation}
It should be emphasized that this represents a first-order approximation, as in Eq.~\ref{eq:corr_cross} the setup-specific detection efficiency $\epsilon_{setup}$ and transmission factor $t_{setup}$ are assumed to be identical for both the empty and target runs. This assumption enables the cancellation of these factors in the ratio.\newline
From the Eq.~\ref{eq:corr_cross} it is evident that for cross section measurements with the transmission method three observables have to be measured:\newline
\begin{enumerate}
\item[$\blacksquare$] \textbf{Number of scattering centers $\mathbf{N_t}$}\newline
The number of scattering centers per unit area of the target is a target specific number. It depends on the target thickness and and its density. The values herefore are taken from~\cite{ponnath2023precise}\footnote{For the purpose of this work the target thicknesses were remeasured at GSI with a chromatic sensor giving 2D depth profiles of each target.}
\begin{enumerate}
\item Thin target:\newline 
target thickness d = 0.5451 cm; $N_t = 5.0588795\cdot 10^{22}$; $\frac{\Delta N_t}{N_t} = 0.0648\%$,
\item Medium target:\newline
target thickness d = 1.0793 cm; $N_t = 1.0016600\cdot 10^{23}$; $\frac{\Delta N_t}{N_t} = 0.2620\%$,
\item Thick target:\newline
target thickness d = 2.1928 cm; $N_t = 2.0350598\cdot 10^{23}$; $\frac{\Delta N_t}{N_t} = 0.0322\%$,
\end{enumerate} 
where $N_t$ was calculated by
\begin{equation}
N_t = \frac{\rho \cdot d \cdot N_A}{M} \, ,
\end{equation}
with $\rho$ the target density\footnote{$\rho = 1.851 g/cm^{3}$, from \cite{ponnath2023precise}}, $N_A$ the Avogadro constant ($6.02214076\cdot10^{23}\,\text{mol}^{-1}$) and $M$ the molar mass of the target (for carbon $M = 12.011\,g \cdot \text{mol}^{-1}$).
\item[$\blacksquare$] \textbf{Number of incoming projectiles ($^{12}$C) $\mathbf{N_1}$}\newline
For the measurement only events with well identified incoming $^{12}$C projectiles are chosen. Herefore strict cuts on the detectors upstream the target area are set. This strict event selection makes sure that we only consider events with single $^{12}$C within the $\pm 4\mu s$ event time window. This will be discussed in more detail in Section~\ref{subsec:event-sel}. 
\item[$\blacksquare$] \textbf{Number of intact projectiles ($^{12}$C) $\mathbf{N_2}$ after the target}\newline
Detectors downstream the target area are used to count the number of intact projectiles $^{12}$C. To reduce detector specific influences which could distort the result it is advisable to use only as few as necessary detectors for the clear identification of intact projectiles and only those with the largest possible efficiency and acceptance. Moreover detector specific efficiencies are canceled out by including both empty and target runs (only in 1st order, as there may be a position dependence) in the cross section calculation (see Eq.~\ref{eq:corr_cross}). For all downstream detectors used in this analysis it is critical to minimize any selection cuts and systematically check their effects on $N_2$.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Charge Changing Cross Section Measurement}\label{subsec:cc_cs}
The charge changing cross section refers to a measure of the probability that the incoming projectile will undergo a reaction inside the target that changes its nuclear charge. To measure the charge changing cross section it can be referred to Eq.~\ref{eq:corr_cross} where in this case $N_2$ is the number of survived carbon isotopes, i.e. projectiles which did not change their charge. For this measurement only the data from the incoming beam and the TWIN MUSIC (see Section~\ref{sec:ionisation_chambers}) are used.\newline
While for the event selection before the target the cut conditions can be arbitrarily strict (it will only have an impact to the statistics and the derived statistical error), cuts on the downstream detectors need to be avoided if at all possible. Too selective cuts on the identification of $N_2$ can alter the result of the measurement.
\subsubsection{TWIN MUSIC Calibration}
For the analysis of data in TWIN MUSIC -- different to the upstream detectors, where calibrated data with default calibration parameters is used -- the so called \textit{mapped} raw level data is processed. In the mapped level TWIN MUSIC provides following information:
\begin{itemize}
\itemsep0em 
\item \textbf{SectionID:} The detector is a double ionization chamber and as such divided into four parts (in beam perspective): section 1 -- right down; section 2 -- right up; section 3 -- left down; section 4 -- left up. For the S444 experiment only section 1 was operated and accordingly geometrically centered on the beam spot.
\item \textbf{AnodeID:} Each section has 16 anodes along the beam line for energy-loss readout and one reference anode (anodeID = 17).
\item \textbf{Time:} Each hit in each anode gets assigned to a time. The drift time (in ns) of the electrons from the ionization process to the anode is calculated by subtracting the individual anode time by the time of the reference channel. The reference anode receives its clean signal from a constant fraction discriminator of the start detector.
\item \textbf{Energy:} Each hit recorded in an anode is assigned a corresponding deposited energy. For the purpose of charged-particle identification in the ionisation chambers, we do not reconstruct the particle charge Z via the first-order relation Z$\sim\Delta E$. Since only carbon nuclei need to be identified, it is sufficient to work directly in the energy-loss ($\Delta E$) space. This avoids the explicit extraction of Z and thus eliminates the square-root transformation, while maintaining unambiguous carbon identification.

\subsubsection{Carbon Identification}\label{subsec:carbon_id}
The most important requirement for measuring absolute cross sections is a clear separation of the intact beam from heavy fragments originating from any reaction in the target or other parts of the setup.\\
The identification of carbon isotopes in TWIN MUSIC is done by a multi-sampling analysis within the segmented detector. This means that the energy loss pattern in one subset of segments must coincide with the pattern in another subset. Since the TWIN MUSIC is multi-hit capable various strategies were developed to deal with multi-hit events, i.e. when having anodes with multiple hits, decide which hit originates from the final state products of the reaction and which from background or noise. It is important to emphasize that these multi-hit events are not associated with pile-up phenomena, as such events are effectively identified due to the longer shaping time of the R3B MUSIC detector and are already excluded by the stringent charge selection applied in the R3B MUSIC data preprocessing.\newline
The default strategy is to use the time information for single hit selection in multiple hit anodes. It has to be remarked that for the S444 experiment the TWIN MUSIC was read out by two independent MDPP modules~\cite{MDPP-16}. The signals from the first reference anode and the first eight upstream anodes were sent to module 1, the ones from the last eight downstream anodes and the second reference anode were forwarded to module 2. For the first eight upstream anodes the drift time is calculated by subtracting the hit time in each anode by the reference time from the first reference anode and for the last eight downstream anodes accordingly the second reference anode was used.\newline
The time based selection algorithm for multiple hit anodes works iteratively:\newline
\begin{enumerate}
\itemsep0em
\item Get a good estimate of the mean drift time for the eight upstream anodes($t_{mean\_up}$) and the eight downstream anodes($t_{mean\_down}$)\footnote{For the case all eight downstream anodes have multiple hits, set $t_{mean\_down} = t_{mean\_up}$ and vice versa}, without considering anodes with multiple hits, typically originating from electronics noise.
\item To determine the real hit for anodes with multiple hits compare the hit time with the according mean drift time ($t_{mean\_up}$ for any of the eight upstream anodes, $t_{mean\_down}$ for any of the eight downstream anodes). Calculate the absolute difference between mean drift time and each hit time
\begin{equation}
\Delta t = | \bar{t} - {t^i}_{drift}|; \, \text{i = anodeID (1-16) with} \quad \bar{t} = 
\begin{cases}
t_{mean\_up} & \text{for i} \leq 8\\
t_{mean\_down} & \text{for i} \geq 9 \\
\end{cases}
\end{equation}
For each anodes with multiple hits select the hit with lowest drift time difference to the mean drift time.
\end{enumerate}

After selecting the appropriate hit for both single- and multi-hit anodes, the mean reconstructed energy loss is calculated separately for the eight upstream anodes ($\overline{\Delta E_{\text{up}}}$) and the eight downstream anodes ($\overline{\Delta E_{\text{down}}}$). To identify the surviving carbon isotopes, a two-dimensional Gaussian fit is then applied to the $\overline{\Delta E_{\text{up}}}$ versus $\overline{\Delta E_{\text{down}}}$ distribution, centered on the peak corresponding to the carbon isotopes (see Fig.~\ref{fig:twin_2d_gaus_cut})
\begin{equation}
f(x) = A e^{-\frac{1}{2}((\frac{x - \bar{x}}{\sigma_{x}})^2 +(\frac{y - \bar{y}}{\sigma_{y}})^2)} \, ,
\label{eq:gauss_fit}
\end{equation}
where $x$ is the mean re-scaled energy loss of the first upstream anodes and $y$ the according eight downstream anodes.\\ 
The number of surviving carbon isotopes -- for both empty and target runs -- is determined by the integral of events within the ellipsoidal contour defined by the 2D Gaussian fit in the ($\overline{\Delta E_{\text{up}}}$ , $\overline{\Delta E_{\text{down}}}$) plane (see Fig.~\ref{fig:twin_2d_gaus_cut}).
Because the upstream and downstream anodes were read out by two independent MDPP modules with slightly different thresholds, some events appear only along one of the two histogram axes (i.e. events for which only the upstream or only the downstream anode produced a registered charge signal). To ensure that these valid carbon events are not lost, the selection is extended as follows:
\begin{itemize}
\item If both upstream and downstream anodes record a hit, the event is counted as a surviving carbon isotope when ($\overline{\Delta E_{\text{up}}}$ , $\overline{\Delta E_{\text{down}}}$) lies inside the 2D Gaussian ellipsoid.
\item If only one of the two anodes records a hit, the event is retained provided that its single recorded charge value lies within the corresponding 1D Gaussian projection of the fitted 2D distribution.
\end{itemize}
A raw estimate of the charge changing cross section, neglecting the influence of the geometrical acceptance of the TWIN MUSIC detector, can be derived from the raw event numbers using Eq.~\ref{eq:corr_cross}
\begin{equation}\label{eq:cs_cc_show}
    \sigma = -\frac{1}{N_t} \ln\left(\frac{N_1^E}{N_2^E} \cdot \frac{N_2}{N_1}\right) \, ,
\end{equation}
where $N_2$ and $N_2^E$ are the numbers of surviving carbon isotopes determined for the target and corresponding empty-target measurement, respectively.\\
Preliminary results are shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:cccs_gaus_with_errors}, where the fit range of the two-dimensional Gaussian was fixed to 3.5 standard deviations (SD) of the Gaussian fit. To determine an optimal and stable fit region, the charge-changing cross section was evaluated systematically for fit ranges between 1 and 5 SD for all beam energies and targets (Fig.~\ref{fig:cccs_vs_sigma_cut}). A broad plateau with minimal dependence of the extracted cross section is observed around 3.5 SD, which is therefore chosen as the standard selection range throughout the analysis.

As shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:cccs_vs_sigma_cut}, the extracted cross sections decrease with increasing beam energy, particularly for large fit ranges. This behaviour is primarily attributed to the change in energy deposition characteristics at higher beam energies. The energy resolution of the TWIN MUSIC detector is  limited by statistical (Poissonian) fluctuations in ionisation, which scale as
\begin{equation*}
\frac{\Delta E}{E} \propto \frac{1}{\sqrt{E}} \, .
\end{equation*}
Moreover, when the beam energy is varied from 400 to 800 AMeV, the measured energy loss in the detector follows an approximate $\frac{1}{\beta^2}$ dependence~\footnote{A detailed description of the energy loss of charged particles via ionisation is provided in Ref.~\cite{kolanoski2016teilchendetektoren}, Section 3.2.}. As a result the deposited energy decreases with increasing beam energy, leading to a degradation of the energy resolution at higher beam energies. This has as consequence that at higher beam energies and for wide fit ranges a non-negligible fraction of boron fragments (Z = 5) may be incorrectly identified as carbon isotopes (Z = 6), due to the diminished peak separation in the energy-loss spectra. This resulting overlap leads then to a systematic overestimation of survived carbon isotopes and thus to an underestimation of the charge changing cross section. This is clearly visible in Fig.~\ref{fig:cccs_vs_sigma_cut} for fit ranges larger than 4.5.\\

\subsection{Geometric Corrections}\label{sec:geo_corr}
During the S444 experiment only sector 1 of the TWIN MUSIC with an active area of $11\times11$ cm$^2$ was used, see Section~\ref{sec:ionisation_chambers}. Particles which undergo elastic or inelastic scattering or simply exit the target at larger angles due to the multiple scattering process, may therefore not be fully confined in the active volume of the TWIN MUSIC. To visualize the restricted geometric acceptance of the TWIN MUSIC the position in $x$ and $y$ (perpendicular plane to the beam direction) on the MWPC1 in front of the ionization chamber was plotted, for comparison without any conditions on the TWIN MUSIC and with the condition of having identified a carbon isotope (with the 2D Gauss-fit method as described in Section~\ref{subsec:carbon_id}), see Fig.~\ref{fig:mw1_xy}. The large active surface area of $20\times20$ cm$^{2}$ of the MWPC1 and its position close to the target affirms a larger acceptance\footnote{This statement does not hold for light fragments as protons or deuterons. Their deflection angle exceeds the geometric acceptance of the MWPC1.} whereas the TWIN MUSIC behind it, with an active surface of $11\times11\text{cm}^{2}$, is -- as mentioned -- not sensitive to the scattered fragments with large deflection angle.\newline
\begin{figure}[htpb]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\textwidth,keepaspectratio=true]{Figures/geo_acceptance_v2.pdf}
    \caption{
    Distribution in $x$ and $y$ on MWPC1 for different energies with and without target. 
     }
    \label{fig:mw1_xy}
\end{figure}

The relative acceptance of the detection system is influenced by both the target thickness and the beam energy. Thicker targets and lower beam energy lead to a broader angular distribution of the fragments due to increased multiple scattering and energy loss, thereby reducing the geometric acceptance. In contrast, higher beam energies result in smaller scattering angles owing to relativistic boost effects, which in turn increase the acceptance. Consequently, the acceptance correction must be evaluated and applied individually for each specific beam-target configuration. It is important to note that acceptance effects cannot be corrected by referencing to empty target runs. All these acceptance effects can be observed in Fig.~\ref{fig:mw1_xy}.\newline
To compensate correctly for the geometric acceptance it has to be considered that for the charge changing cross section measurement only the carbon isotopes after the target are counted in the TWIN MUSIC. Therefore the correction should only be applied to the carbon isotopes (Z = 6) on the $x$-$y$ distribution on the MWPC1, see Fig.~\ref{fig:mw1_xy}.\\
A precise correction for the limited geometric acceptance of the TWIN MUSIC detector presents a notable challenge, as the true distribution of carbon fragments in the $x$- and $y$- coordinates at the position of MWPC1 is not directly measurable. The expected true distribution results from a convolution of the primary $^{12}C$ beam profile and the Gaussian-like angular broadening caused by multiple scattering processes within the target material and the reaction dynamics.\\
To reconstruct fragment tracks extending beyond the geometrical acceptance of the TWIN MUSIC detector, a graphical symmetry-based method was applied to the $x$- and $y$- distributions separately. This method avoids the instability and parameter sensitivity of complex fit-based extrapolations. Instead, a binwise comparison approach was used, as described below:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Estimation of the symmetry center:}  
    A first-order estimate of the central value (mean) of the $x$ and $y$ distributions is determined.

    \item \textbf{Mirroring of the unaffected distribution part:}  
    The area of the distribution assumed to be unaffected by acceptance losses is mirrored across the estimated center. For the $x$-distribution, the right-hand side is flipped onto the left; for the $y$-distribution, the low part is mirrored onto the top part. The resulting $x$-mirrored distribution for one exemplary beam-target configuration is shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:geo_correction} (orange histogram), overlaid on the full measured distribution (blue histogram).

    \item \textbf{Fine-tuning of the mirror axis:}  
    As the distributions are not perfectly symmetric, the estimated mirror axis is iteratively adjusted to optimize agreement in the central, undistorted region.

    \item \textbf{Bin-by-bin ratio analysis:}  
    The flipped and measured distributions are compared on a bin-by-bin basis. As illustrated in Fig.~\ref{fig:geo_correction}, lower panel, the ratio between the two distributions drops significantly around $x \approx -20\,\text{mm}$, indicating the onset of acceptance loss. This reduced level persists down to approximately $x \approx -25\,\text{mm}$.

    \item \textbf{Calculation of the correction factor:}  
    To estimate the number of missing hits due to limited acceptance, the bin contents of both the flipped and measured distributions are summed over the range from the edge of the active area of MWPC1 ($x = -110\,\text{mm}$) up to the observed acceptance drop (e.g. $x = -20\,\text{mm}$). The correction factor is then computed as
    \begin{equation}
        \text{geo}_{x} = 1 + \frac{\sum_{x = -110}^{-20} (h_{\text{flipped}}[x] - h_{\text{full}}[x])}{\sum_{x = -110}^{ +110} h_{\text{full}}[x]} \, ,
    \end{equation}
    where $h_{\text{full}}[x]$ is the bin-wise histogram content of the full measured histogram (blue histogram in Fig.~\ref{fig:geo_correction}) and $h_{\text{flipped}}[x]$ the according mirrored distribution (orange histogram in Fig.~\ref{fig:geo_correction}). 
	The final value of $\text{geo}_x$ is taken as the average of the results obtained at the two boundaries of the acceptance-drop region ($x = -25\text{mm}$ and $x = -20\text{mm}$). The uncertainty ($\Delta x$ or $\Delta y$) is estimated from the spread between the minimum, maximum, and mean values in this interval.

    \item \textbf{Application to the $y$-direction:}  
    The same procedure is applied independently to the $y$-distribution, yielding the corresponding correction factor $\text{geo\_corr}_y$.
\end{enumerate}
The final geometric correction factor, accounting for both spatial directions, is therefore expressed as follows and is shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:geo_corrections_overview}
\begin{equation}
    \text{geo}_{xy} = \text{geo}_x \cdot \text{geo}_y \, .
\end{equation}
\begin{figure}[htbp]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{Figures/geo_corr_flipping_thick_target_400amev.png}
    \caption{Top: Measured $x$-distribution (blue) and mirrored right-hand side distribution (orange) for MWPC1. Bottom: Bin-by-bin ratio between the two distributions. The sudden drop at $x \approx 20\,\text{mm}$ indicates the onset of significant acceptance loss.}
    \label{fig:geo_correction}
\end{figure}
The described correction procedure must be systematically applied to all target and empty runs for each beam energy. As the onset and extent of geometric distortion may vary with beam conditions, the integration ranges used in the binwise summation must be adapted accordingly on a case-by-case basis.\\
\begin{figure}[htbp]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[height=8cm,keepaspectratio=true]{Figures/geo_corr_factors_flipping.png}
    \caption{Overview of the geometric acceptance correction factors $\text{geo}_{xy}$.}
    \label{fig:geo_corrections_overview}
\end{figure}
An overview of the resulting correction factors $\text{geo}_{xy}$ is presented in Fig.~\ref{fig:geo_corrections_overview}, according to target thickness and beam energy. The error bars reflect the uncertainty in the estimated onset of acceptance loss in both the x- and y-distributions. Specifically, they are derived from the variation of the correction factor when shifting the integration bounds between, e.g., $x$=-25 mm and $x$=-20 mm (and analogously in $y$).

Once computed, the geometric correction factors can be directly included in the calculation of the charge-changing cross section via Eq.~\ref{eq:corr_cross}. As they appear inside the logarithmic term, the multiplicative correction factors translate into additive contributions for full (geo$_{xy}^{\text{T}}$) and empty (geo$_{xy}^{\text{E}}$) target
\begin{equation}
\sigma_{\text{corr}} = -\frac{1}{N_t} \ln(\text{geo}_{xy}^{\text{T}}) + \frac{1}{N_t} \ln(\text{geo}_{xy}^{\text{E}}) \, .
\label{eq:geo_corr_sigma}
\end{equation}
The correction term $\sigma_{\text{corr}}$ accounts for the number of undetected carbon isotopes (Z = 6) after the target due to the limited geometric acceptance of the TWIN MUSIC.\\
The aforementioned errors on the geometric correction factors are propagated to the charge changing cross section correction factor $\sigma_{\text{corr}}$ in Eq.~\ref{eq:geo_corr_sigma} as
\begin{equation}
\Delta_{sys}\sigma_{\text{corr}} = \frac{1}{N_t} \cdot \sqrt{ \left(\frac{\Delta x^T}{\text{geo}_{x}^T} \right)^2 + \left(\frac{\Delta x^E}{\text{geo}_{x}^E} \right)^2 + \left(\frac{\Delta y^T}{\text{geo}_{y}^T} \right)^2 + \left(\frac{\Delta y^E}{\text{geo}_{y}^E} \right)^2 } \, , 
\end{equation}
where $\Delta x$ and $\Delta y$ are the factor's uncertainties within the acceptance-drop region. The superscripts $T/E$ refer to the target and empty runs accordingly. \\ 
The negative correction term $\sigma_{\text{corr}}$ is then added to the preliminary estimate of the charge-changing cross section to yield the final result
\begin{equation}
\sigma_{\text{cc}}^{\text{final}} = \sigma_{\text{cc}}^{\text{raw}} + \sigma_{\text{corr}} \, .
\end{equation}
Fig.~\ref{fig:cccs_gaus_with_errors} shows the raw estimates before applying geometric acceptance corrections, while the fully corrected charge-changing cross sections are presented in Fig.~\ref{fig:cccs_xy_corrected}.\\
As expected significantly affected by the  geometric correction are runs with 400 and 550 AMeV beam energy whereas at beam energies of 650 and 800 AMeV the effect is exceptionally small since at high beam energies  the fragments after the target perceive a strong boost effect in beam direction which confines the distribution in the x-y-plane within the active area of the TWIN MUSIC.

\subsection{Isotope Correction}\label{sec:isotope_corr}
For the determination of the total interaction cross section, it is necessary to identify not only the surviving carbon fragments, as done in Subsection~\ref{subsec:cc_cs} for the charge-changing cross section, but specifically the surviving $^{12}$C isotopes. This ensures that the number of neutrons in the projectile remains unchanged. Consequently, an unambiguous mass identification of the carbon isotopes is required.\\
The R3B setup provides, in principle, full charge and mass identification of reaction fragments behind the target, utilizing tracking detectors (e.g., MWPCs), time-of-flight detectors (e.g., START, SofiaToF Wall), and charge measurement systems (e.g., TWIN MUSIC). These measurements are combined through the magnetic rigidity relation
\begin{equation}\label{eq:mag_rig}
B\cdot \rho = \frac{\gamma \cdot m \cdot v}{q} \, ,
\end{equation}
where $B$ is the magnetic field component perpendicular to the particle trajectory, $\rho$ is the curvature radius of the trajectory in the magnetic field, $\gamma$ is the Lorentz factor, $v$ is the particle velocity. This relation allows extraction of the mass-to-charge ratio m/q and thus the isotope.\\
However, in the present case, a direct mass determination using the standard R3B time-of-flight and tracking information was not feasible. The SofiaToF  Wall detector, optimized for nuclei with Z $\geq$ 20, exhibits substantially reduced efficiency for carbon (Z = 6), and downstream of GLAD only a single tracking detector (MWPC3) was available. As a result, the reconstruction of fragment velocity and full trajectory with sufficient precision could not be performed.\\
Instead, the mass identification of the carbon fragments was achieved through the correlation of the $x$-positions measured in MWPC2 (upstream of GLAD) and MWPC3 (downstream). Due to the magnetic deflection in GLAD, fragments with different A/q values follow distinct trajectories, leading to clearly separated bands in the (x$_{MWPC2}$,x$_{MWPC3}$) correlation plane. The difference in A/q between neighboring carbon isotopes is sufficiently large to enable a reliable isotope separation.\\
Fig.~\ref{sfig:mw23x_def} shows the correlation of the $x$ positions measured in MWPC2 and MWPC3 for fragments with atomic number Z = 6 detected in the 20~mm target run at a beam energy of 400~AMeV. The dominant diagonal structure corresponds to $^{12}$C fragments, which, due to their higher mass-to-charge ratio $(A/Z)$ compared to lighter carbon isotopes, experience the smallest deflection in the GLAD magnetic field. Below this main correlation line, a second, less intense band corresponds to $^{11}$C, and a small number of events near the lower edge is identified as $^{10}$C fragments.

The horizontal and vertical bands around $x \approx 0$ and $y \approx 0$ arise from misreconstructed positions in the MWPCs, which can occur in the presence of multiple low-amplitude signals. To address this artifact, a coherent clustering algorithm was developed (see Appendix~\ref{app:coh_cluster}) that selects spatially localized clusters of charge signals. This method was also applied in the geometric acceptance correction described in Section~\ref{sec:geo_corr}. Fig.~\ref{sfig:mw23x_coh} illustrates the reconstruction refinement achieved through this approach, together with correlated cuts on the $x$  and $y$ positions measured by MWPC1 and MWPC2, which lead to a reduction of the uncorrelated bands by more than 90\%.

Despite these improvements, both reconstruction methods reveal a truncation of the correlation plot at low $x$ values in MWPC2. As discussed in detail in Section~\ref{sec:geo_corr}, this cutoff originates from the limited geometric acceptance of the TWIN MUSIC detector. Since lighter reaction fragments such as $^{11}$C and $^{10}$C exhibit a broader momentum distribution compared to the intact $^{12}$C beam particles, they are more affected by the acceptance limitations. Consequently, extracting isotope ratios directly from the uncorrected 2D correlation (e.g., Fig~\ref{sfig:mw23x_coh}) would introduce a systematic bias.

To mitigate this effect, a dedicated procedure was implemented:

\begin{itemize}
\item The MWPC1 $x$-$y$ distribution of the carbon isotopes of interest is partitioned into four regions as shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:iso_mw1_sections}: the central region (a), the middle-right region (b), the middle-lower region (c), and the lower-right region (d).
\item The MWPC2 vs. MWPC3 $x$ correlation is recalculated for each region, applying a differential event weighting: region (a) is weighted by 1, regions (b) and (c) by 2, and region (d) by 4. Events with hits outside these regions (upper left corner) are omitted. The idea behind the partitioning is to  substitute the upper left side of the distribution -- affected by the limited geometric acceptance -- by the lower right side  (region (b), (c) and (d)), assuming a symmetric distribution in both $x$ and $y$ with respect to the central region (a). 
\item The prominent $^{12}$C correlation band is then fitted with a linear function to extract its slope (Fig.~\ref{sfig:mw23x_central_fit}).
\item Based on the fitted slope, the histogram is rotated such that the $^{12}$C band is vertically aligned. A 1D projection onto the abscissa is performed (Fig.~\ref{sfig:mw23x_rot_and_proj}).
\item Finally, the resulting 1D distribution is fitted -- see Section~\ref{sec:distr_fitting} -- to extract the relative yield of $^{12}$C with respect to the total Z = 6 fragment population in the $x$ MWPC2-MWPC3 correlation space.
\end{itemize}
This procedure is carried out for all analysed runs separately.

\subsubsection{Distribution fitting}\label{sec:distr_fitting}
The fitting procedure of the one-dimensional distribution described in the previous section is performed in two steps:
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\roman*)]
    \item In the first step, the left side of the distribution, which is dominated by the contribution from $^{12}\mathrm{C}$ fragments, is fitted in the range $[-150, 10]~\mathrm{mm}$. The fit is performed using the sum of a Voigt profile---defined as the convolution of a Cauchy--Lorentz and a Gaussian distribution~\footnote{The implementation of the Voigt profile in the ROOT framework is based on the FORTRAN code presented in~\cite{wells1999rapid}}---and an additional Gaussian function: the Voigt profile accounts for the convolution of beam-related Gaussian broadening with Lorentzian tails arising from rare large-angle Coulomb scattering at small impact parameter and from multi nucleon–nucleon scattering, while the additional Gaussian component represents the intrinsic spatial resolution of the tracking detectors and the multiple electron scattering.

    \item In the second step, a global fit of the full distribution is performed. In this fit, the parameters of the Voigt profile and Gaussian function obtained from step (i) are kept fixed. The peaks corresponding to $^{11}\mathrm{C}$ and $^{10}\mathrm{C}$ are modeled by Gaussian functions. The only constraint is applied to the peak widths: the width of the \({}^{10}\mathrm{C}\) peak is allowed to vary within \(\pm 20\%\) around a value larger by a factor of \(\sqrt{2}\) compared to the \({}^{11}\mathrm{C}\) peak width. The factor of \(\sqrt{2}\) accounts for the expected broadening associated with the removal of two neutrons. 
\end{enumerate}
An example of the resulting combined fit is shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:example_histo_fit} for the medium-target run at a beam energy of 400~AMeV. A complete set of fits for all analyzed runs is provided in Appendix~\ref{app:fit_carbon}.\\

The correction factor accounting for the isotopic composition of the carbon fragments is then defined as
\begin{equation}
    c_{\mathrm{iso}} \;=\; \frac{N_{12\mathrm{C}}}{N_{\mathrm{C}}} \, ,
\end{equation}
where $N_{12\mathrm{C}}$ denotes the number of reconstructed $^{12}\mathrm{C}$ fragments obtained by integrating the Voigt profile and the Gaussian component from step (i) over the full range. The data distribution was fitted using a Gaussian to account for inelastic excitations of $^{12}$C (E$^*$ $\le$ S$_n$), together with a Voigt profile to describe the observed broading in the MWPCs. The resulting fit reproduces the observed peak shape, as shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:example_histo_fit}. The total number of reconstructed carbon fragments, $N_{\mathrm{C}}$, is determined by integrating the full combined fit, i.e.\ the sum of $N_{12\mathrm{C}}, N_{11\mathrm{C}}$, and $N_{10\mathrm{C}}$, over the same range.\newline
The contribution of this isotopic correction to the total interaction cross section can be obtained by applying the logarithmic term used in the cross section calculation. Specifically, the isotopic correction cross section is given by
\begin{equation}
\sigma_{\text{iso}} = -\frac{1}{N_t} \ln(c_{\text{iso}}^T) + -\frac{1}{N_t} \ln(c_{\text{iso}}^E) \, ,
\end{equation}
where $c_{\text{iso}}^T$ and $c_{\text{iso}}^E$ are the isotopic correction factors for the target and empty runs, respectively, and $N_t$ is the number of target nuclei per unit area.

The black diamonds represent cross section predictions from EPAX 3.1a, a universal empirical parametrization for fragmentation cross sections in high-energy heavy-ion reactions~\cite{summerer2012improved}, as implemented in the LISE++ simulation framework~\cite{tarasov2008lise++}.}
    \label{fig:iso_corr_contribution}
\end{figure}
The numerical contribution of the isotopic correction to the total cross section for the various target thicknesses is presented in Fig.~\ref{fig:iso_corr_contribution}. The corresponding measured values for $c_{\text{iso}}^T$ and $c_{\text{iso}}^E$ are summarized in Table~\ref{tab:cs_contr}.\\
The observed differences in the contributions for the respective target thicknesses originate from efficiency uncertainties and position-dependent effects in MWPC3. The variation in energy loss depending on the target thickness leads to small variations in the magnetic deflection. Consequently, the reconstructed $x$-position on MWPC3 shifts slightly with target thickness. \newline
However, no systematic shifts related to the target thickness is observed  in Fig.~\ref{fig:iso_corr_contribution}. This indicates that secondary scattering or reaction effects are negligible for the target thicknesses used in this experiment.\newline
To account for systematic effects associated with detector response and target positioning, a weighted arithmetic mean was calculated for each beam energy, using the three data points obtained for the different target thicknesses. The weights are defined according to the variances, such that the weight assigned to data point $i$ is given by
\begin{equation}
w_i = \frac{1}{\text{Var}(\sigma_{iso,i})} \, ,
\end{equation}
where $\text{Var}(\sigma_{iso,i})$ denotes the variance of the isotope cross section contribution of data point $i$. The resulting weighted mean of the isotope cross section contribution is then defined as
\begin{equation}
\bar{\sigma}_{iso,i} = \frac{\sum_i w_i \sigma_{iso,i}}{\sum_i w_i} \, ,
\end{equation}
where the summation over $i$ extends over the three target thicknesses. The resulting weighted means for each beam energy are shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:iso_corr_weighted_mean}. 
%The error bars represent the standard error of the weighted mean, which is given by 
The total uncertainty of the combined measurement consists of two contributions.
First, the statistical uncertainty associated with the weighted mean is given by
\begin{equation}
\Delta_w = \sqrt{\frac{1}{\sum_i w_i}} \, .
\end{equation}
Second, to account for the observed spread of the measurements, an additional systematic scatter term is estimated through the weighted sample variance
\begin{equation}
s_w^2 = \frac{\sum_i w_i \left( x_i - \bar{x}_w \right)^2}{\sum_i w_i} \, .
\end{equation}
The final uncertainty on the combined result is obtained by adding both contributions
in quadrature
\begin{equation}
\Delta \bar{\sigma}_{iso} = \sqrt{\Delta_w^2 + s_w^2} \, .
\end{equation}

\subsection{Statistical and Systematic Error Analysis}\label{sec:err_analysis}
All measurements presented in this Chapter, rely on the transmission method and the error analysis of those measurements are treated the same accordingly. The generic formula for the measurements was presented in Section~\ref{section:transmission_method}, Eq.~\ref{eq:cross_sec}.\newline
For mutually independent quantities, the Gaussian error propagation for statistical and systematical uncertainties is given by
\begin{equation}
\Delta_{stat./syst.}  = \sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^n \left( \frac{\partial f}{\partial x_i} \cdot \Delta_{x_i} \right)^2 } \, ,
\end{equation}
\text{with}
\begin{align*}
	f(x_1,x_2,..,x_n) &\quad \text{the error prone cross section function,} \\
	x_i  &\quad \text{the independent variables ($N_1$,$N_2$,$N_t$; see Eq.~\ref{eq:cross_sec}),} \\
	\Delta_{x_i} &\quad \text{stat./syst. uncertainties associated to independent variable $x_i$.}
\end{align*}
Hereafter, the superscript $^E$ denotes quantities obtained from the empty-target runs, while the superscript $^T$ refers to those measured with the target in place.
All three measurements presented in this section, the preliminary estimate charge changing cross section, the geometric correction factor geo$_{\text{xy}}$ and the isotope correction factor $c_{iso}$, finally contribute to the total interaction cross section
\begin{equation}\label{eq:sigma_R}
\sigma_R = -\frac{1}{N_t}ln\left(\frac{N^T_2}{N^T_1} \, \frac{N^E_1}{N^E_2} \cdot \frac{\text{geo}^T_{\text{xy}}}{\text{geo}^E_{\text{xy}}} \cdot \frac{c^T_{iso}}{c^E_{iso}}\right) \, ,
\end{equation}
where $N_1$ are the number of incoming $^{12}C$ ions and $N_2$ are the number of survived carbon isotopes for target and empty runs respectively, as described in Section~\ref{subsec:carbon_id}.\\
This expression is valid under the assumption that the detection efficiency of the TWIN MUSIC for carbon fragments, as well as the DAQ dead time, are identical in the full and empty target runs. Under this condition, the corresponding efficiency corrections cancel in the fracture term in Eq.~\ref{eq:sigma_R}.

\subsubsection{Charge-Changing Cross Section}

\textbf{Statistical Uncertainty} \\
The statistical uncertainty associated with the charge-changing cross section, after applying geometry corrections, includes contributions from the standard error of binomially distributed variables.
The charge-changing cross section is derived from survival probabilities of carbon isotopes.
The survival probability $P_{\text{surv}|\text{cc}}$, defined as the probability of observing a carbon isotope in the final state, is based on $N_1$ independent trials with binary outcomes (survived or not). The survival probability $P_{\text{surv}|\text{cc}}$ is given by the ratio $N_2 / N_1$ and its uncertainty derived from binomial statistics
\begin{equation}\label{eq:surv_prob}
\Delta P_{\text{surv}|\text{cc}} = \sqrt{ \frac{P_{\text{surv}|\text{cc}} \cdot \left(1 - P_{\text{surv}|\text{cc}}\right)}{N_1} } \, .
\end{equation}
The total statistical uncertainty is given by
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:stat_cc_}
\Delta\sigma_{\text{stat}|\text{cc}} = \sqrt{\left(\frac{\partial \sigma_{\text{cc}}}{\partial P^{T}_{\text{surv}|\text{cc}}} \Delta P^{T}_{\text{surv}|\text{cc}}\right)^2 + \left(\frac{\partial \sigma_{\text{cc}}}{\partial P^{E}_{\text{surv}|\text{cc}}} \Delta P^{E}_{\text{surv}|\text{cc}}\right)^2} \, .
\end{equation}
By inserting Eq.~\ref{eq:surv_prob} in Eq.~\ref{eq:stat_cc_} we obtain
\begin{equation}
\Delta\sigma_{\text{stat}|\text{cc}} = \sqrt{ \frac{1}{N_t^2} \left( \frac{1 - P^{T}_{\text{surv}|\text{cc}}}{P^{T}_{\text{surv}|\text{cc}} N^{T}_1} + \frac{1 - P^{E}_{\text{surv}|\text{cc}}}{P^{E}_{\text{surv}|\text{cc}} N^{E}_1} \right) } \, .
\end{equation}


\textbf{Systematic Uncertainty} \\
The systematic uncertainty comprises the following contributions:
\begin{itemize}
  \item Uncertainty $\Delta N_t$ in the number of target nuclei due to thickness measurement uncertainties.
  \item Dead-time effects and related corrections, see Appendix~\ref{app:twin_uncert}, quantified by $\Delta^{\text{sys}}_{N_2}$.
  \item Uncertainties in the geometrical correction factor $\text{geo}_{xy}$, see Section~\ref{sec:geo_corr}, contributing $\Delta_{\text{sys}}\sigma_{\text{corr}}$.
\end{itemize}

The total systematic uncertainty is then expressed as
\begin{equation}
\Delta\sigma_{\text{sys}|\text{cc}} = \sqrt{ \left( \frac{\partial \sigma_{\text{cc}}}{\partial N_t} \Delta N_t \right)^2 + \left( \Delta^{\text{sys}}_{N_2} \right)^2 + \left( \Delta_{\text{sys}}\sigma_{\text{corr}} \right)^2 } \, .
\end{equation}

\subsubsection{Isotope Correction Contribution}\label{sec:iso_corr}

\textbf{Statistical Uncertainty} \\
The statistical uncertainty of the isotope correction includes contributions from (i) the statistical uncertainty of the charge-changing cross section and (ii) the standard error associated with the binomial distribution describing the number of detected $^{12}$C isotopes relative to the total number of identified carbon fragments (i.e., the total number of entries in Fig.~\ref{fig:example_histo_fit}). The corresponding survival probability is defined as
\begin{equation}
P_{\text{surv}|^{12}\mathrm{C}} = \frac{N_{^{12}\mathrm{C}}}{N_C} \, ,
\end{equation}
where $N_{^{12}\mathrm{C}}$ is the number of detected $^{12}$C isotopes and $N_C$ the total number of identified carbon fragments.\\
Analogous to the derivation of the statistical uncertainty of the charge-changing cross section, the statistical uncertainty of the survival probability is obtained as
\begin{equation}
\Delta\sigma_{\text{stat}|\:^{12}\mathrm{C}} = \sqrt{ \frac{1}{N_t^2} \left( \frac{1 - P^{T}_{\text{surv}|^{12}\mathrm{C}}}{P^{T}_{\text{surv}|^{12}\mathrm{C}} N^{T}_C} + \frac{1 - P^{E}_{\text{surv}|^{12}\mathrm{C}}}{P^{E}_{\text{surv}|^{12}\mathrm{C}} N^{E}_C} \right) } \, .
\end{equation}
Combining with the charge-changing uncertainty yields the total statistical uncertainty of the isotope correction contribution
\begin{equation}
\Delta\sigma_{\text{stat}|\:\text{iso}} = \sqrt{ \frac{1}{N_t^2} \left( \frac{1 - P^{T}_{\text{surv}|cc}}{P^{T}_{\text{surv}|cc} N^{T}_1} + \frac{1 - P^{E}_{\text{surv}|cc}}{P^{E}_{\text{surv}|cc} N_1^E} \right) + \frac{1}{N_t^2} \left( \frac{1 - P^{T}_{\text{surv}|^{12}\mathrm{C}}}{P^{T}_{\text{surv}|^{12}\mathrm{C}} N_C} + \frac{1 - P^{E}_{\text{surv}|^{12}\mathrm{C}}}{P^{E}_{\text{surv}|^{12}\mathrm{C}} N^{E}_C} \right) } \, .
\end{equation}

The systematic uncertainty of the isotope cross section includes contributions from:

\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
    \item the systematic uncertainty of the charge-changing cross section, and
    \item the uncertainty associated with the isotope ratio obtained from the fit of the $^{12}\mathrm{C}$ peak, $\Delta N_{^{12}\mathrm{C}}$, shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:example_histo_fit}. From this fit, the number of surviving $^{12}\mathrm{C}$ isotopes is extracted in order to determine the correction factor $c_{\text{iso}} = N_{^{12}\mathrm{C}} / N_{\mathrm{C}}$. The uncertainty $\Delta N_{^{12}\mathrm{C}}$ is derived from the propagated errors of the fitted peak parameters using the covariance matrix of the fit (see Section~\ref{sec:isotope_corr}).
\end{enumerate}

The total systematic uncertainty is therefore given by
\begin{equation}
\Delta\sigma_{\text{sys}|\:\text{iso}} = \sqrt{
    \left( \frac{\partial \sigma_{cc}}{\partial N_t}\, \Delta N_t \right)^2
    + \left( \Delta^{\text{sys}}_{N_2} \right)^2
    + \left( \Delta_{\text{sys}} \sigma_{\text{corr}} \right)^2
    + \frac{1}{N_t^2} \left( \frac{\Delta N_{^{12}\mathrm{C}}}{N_{^{12}\mathrm{C}}} \right)^2 } \, .
\end{equation}

\subsubsection{Total Interaction Cross Section}

The statistical and systematic uncertainties associated with the total interaction cross section are identical in form to those described for the isotope correction contribution. They include contributions from both the charge-changing cross section and the isotopic correction procedure, as detailed in the previous section.

\subsection{ Results and Discussion}
The S444 experiment -- although originally conceived as a commissioning run, during which several detector systems were being tested for the subsequent experimental campaign S467 on Ca isotopes -- already enabled highly successful studies of the $^{12}$C+$^{12}$C reaction. The analysis particularly benefited from the large geometrical acceptance of all downstream detectors, which not only facilitated the central observable, the measurement of the total interaction cross section, but also permitted the determination of the charge-changing cross section as well as the isotope correction.\newline
The results for the total interaction and charge-changing cross sections obtained in this work are presented in Fig.~\ref{fig:final_res_cc_ti}, together with results from previous experiments (Ponnath et al.~\cite{ponnath2024measurement}) and theoretical predictions (Bertulani et al.~\cite{bertulani2010pauli,teixeira2022nuclear}). As a stringent consistency check, the extracted cross sections are shown for all three target thicknesses employed. Within uncertainties, the three independent measurements agree entirely, thereby demonstrating the robustness and reliability of the applied analysis methods.\newline
The precise numerical values of the total interaction cross sections are summarized in Table~\ref{tab:fin_res}, including both systematic and statistical uncertainties. The statistical component dominates, especially for the thinnest targets, as expected. Nevertheless, the achieved precision is remarkable: with uncertainties reaching down to 0.3\%, the R3B setup enabled high-accuracy measurements of fundamental scattering observables.\newline
Such precision is of considerable importance, as it provides stringent benchmarks for nuclear reaction models and thereby contributes directly to constraining key parameters of the nuclear Equation of State, in particular the slope parameter L of the symmetry energy. The success of this commissioning run thus underlines both the capability of the experimental setup and the scientific relevance of the present measurements.\newline

Possible explanations for the observed quenching of the total interaction cross section in the present work, as well as in the previous measurements by Ponnath et al.~\cite{ponnath2024measurement}, have been discussed in that reference and are further considered here. One potential origin is the clusterization of carbon nuclei into $\alpha$-clusters. Such cluster structures were first predicted and experimentally confirmed in the 1950s, most notably through the identification of the so-called Hoyle state~\cite{hoyle1954nuclear,FREER20141}. These in-medium structural modifications, which are not yet incorporated into the reaction models employed in this work, could lead to a reduction of the total interaction cross section at higher beam energies.\newline
