\section{Nuclear Scattering Reactions in the Glauber Framework}\label{sec:reac_model}
Nuclear scattering reactions are central tools for the investigation of the nature of the structure and dynamics of nuclei since the discovery of the nucleus. In 1911 Rutherford, Geiger and Marsden investigated the nature of gold atoms by bombarding them with alpha particles. By their surprise they noticed that the majority of the alpha particles passed through the gold foil, but few were scattered at large angles. This observation of backward scattered particles forced Rutherford to revise the Thomson atom model and the development of the Rutherford atom model with the mass and positive charge concentrated in a small volume, the so called nucleus, 10$^5$ smaller than the atom as a whole~\cite{rutherford1911lxxix}.\\
Since then, nuclear scattering experiments have evolved into indispensable tools for probing nuclear properties across a vast range of energies and momentum transfers. This section focuses on high-energy scattering reactions, which provide unique access to the internal nucleonic degrees of freedom and enable the study of nuclear structure on short-distance scales. This is primarily facilitated by the inverse relationship between the de Broglie wavelength ($\lambda$) and the incident particle momentum. At high energies, the reduced de Broglie wavelength allows for the spatial resolution of short distances revealing details beyond the macroscopic properties of nuclei.\\
%A significant distinction of pure nuclear scattering experiments, in contrast to electron-nucleus scattering, lies in their ability to directly probe the nuclear force. This permits the detailed investigation of short-range correlations (SRCs) between nucleons, which are crucial for understanding the nuclear equation of state.\\
High-energy nuclear scattering reactions serve a variety of purposes in nuclear and astroparticle physics, including:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Investigation of Ground State Properties:} They provide insights into binding energies, sizes, and shapes of nuclei in their ground states.
    \item \textbf{Quasi-Free Scattering:} Accessing the quasi-free scattering regime (further detailed in Section \ref{sec:qfs_theo}) which enables mapping of single-particle properties inside the nuclei.
    \item \textbf{Inelastic Excitations and Resonances:} These experiments are instrumental in studying inelastic excitations and nuclear resonances, including collective modes of nuclei and the determination of transition form factors.
    \item \textbf{Connection to Fundamental QCD Degrees of Freedom:} At sufficiently high incident energies, scattering reactions can resolve sub-nucleonic structures, providing a direct link to the fundamental quark and gluon degrees of freedom of Quantum Chromodynamics (QCD).
\end{itemize}

An comprehensive overview of various nuclear reactions, including scattering reactions, categorized by impact parameter (related to the centrality of the collision, as discussed in the subsequent subsection) and incident energy, is presented in Fig.~\ref{fig:NuclearReactionsOverview}. In the medium-energy regime, typically ranging from several tens of AMeV (MeV per nucleon) up to 1 AGeV, scattering reactions can encompass a wide spectrum of processes. These range from fragmentation and complete disintegration into multiple smaller particles at small impact parameters to single nucleon knockout reactions and Coulomb excitations at larger impact parameters.\\

Given the short interaction time characteristic of the high-energy regime, these reactions are often interpreted as singular nuclear interactions and are thus termed \textit{direct reactions}. Direct reactions encompass several key channels, which can be expressed in short notation (see also Ref.~\cite{obertelli2021modern}):
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Elastic Scattering:} $a + A \rightarrow a + A$, denoted as $A(a, a)A$. In this process, the projectile $a$ scatters off the target nucleus $A$ without any change in their internal states.
    \item \textbf{Inelastic Scattering:} $a + A \rightarrow a' + A^*$, denoted as $A(a, a')A^*$. Here, the projectile $a$ excites the target nucleus $A$ to an excited state $A^*$, resulting in a change in the internal energy of the target.
    \item \textbf{Particle Transfer Reaction:} $a + A \rightarrow b + B$, denoted as $A(a, b)B$. This involves the exchange of nucleons or clusters of nucleons between the projectile and target, leading to new particles $b$ and $B$.
    \item \textbf{Breakup/Knockout Reaction:} $a + A \rightarrow b + X$, denoted as $A(a, b)X$. In these reactions, one or more nucleons are ejected from the target nucleus, leaving behind a remnant $X$, often accompanied by the projectile $b$.
\end{itemize}

The accurate interpretation of such high-energy nuclear reactions necessitates a robust theoretical framework capable of addressing complex phenomena such as multiple scattering processes and nuclear transparency effects, particularly when dealing with complex nuclei. The Glauber model, originally formulated in the 1950s, provides an established and widely utilized framework for this purpose.\\
Before introducing the fundamental concepts of scattering theory within the Glauber framework, the key integrated cross sections relevant for this work are briefly defined:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \boldmath$\sigma_{\mathrm{el}}$\unboldmath{} -- \textit{Total elastic cross section}: The projectile remains in its ground state, and no nucleon is removed.
    
    \item \boldmath$\sigma_{\mathrm{inel}}$\unboldmath{} -- \textit{Total inelastic cross section (bound excitations)}: The projectile is excited to a bound state without nucleon removal.
    
    \item \boldmath$\sigma_{\mathrm{I}}$\unboldmath{} -- \textit{Total interaction cross section}: The projectile undergoes a transformation involving the removal of at least one nucleon.
\end{itemize}
Two additional composite cross sections are defined
\begin{align}
    \sigma_{\mathrm{R}} &= \sigma_{\mathrm{inel}} + \sigma_{\mathrm{I}} \quad \text{(Total reaction cross section)} \, , \\
    \sigma_{\mathrm{T}} &= \sigma_{\mathrm{el}} + \sigma_{\mathrm{R}} = \sigma_{\mathrm{el}} + \sigma_{\mathrm{inel}} + \sigma_{\mathrm{I}} \quad \text{(Total cross section)} \, . \label{eq:tot_cs_def}
\end{align}
At high beam energies ($E_{\mathrm{beam}} > 200$~AMeV), the contribution from inelastic nucleon-nucleon reaction channels is small. This allows the approximation
\begin{equation}
    \sigma_{\mathrm{R}} \approx \sigma_{\mathrm{I}} \, .
\end{equation}
The interaction cross section $\sigma_{\mathrm{I}}$ can be further decomposed according to the type of nucleon removed
\begin{align}
    \sigma_{\mathrm{I}} &= \sigma_{\Delta Z} + \sigma_{\Delta N} \, , 
\end{align}
where:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \boldmath$\sigma_{\Delta Z}$\unboldmath{} -- \textit{Total charge-changing cross section}: At least one proton is removed from the projectile, such that $Z_i \neq Z_f$.
    
    \item \boldmath$\sigma_{\Delta N}$\unboldmath{} -- \textit{Total neutron-removal cross section}: At least one neutron is removed while the charge remains unchanged ($N_i \neq N_f$, $Z_i = Z_f$).
\end{itemize}
Here, the indices $i$ and $f$ denote the initial and final states of the projectile, respectively.\\
Further details on the experimental determination of these cross sections are provided in Section~\ref{section:transmission_method}.\\
The subsequent subsections will briefly introduce the main concepts and essential ingredients of scattering theory within the Glauber framework. A comprehensive and detailed theoretical derivation of the formalism, while foundational, falls outside the scope of this thesis. For a thorough introduction to these concepts, the reader is referred to the standard lecture by Glauber~\cite{glauber1959lectures} and other standard works~\cite{sitenko1990theory,lilley2013nuclear,bertulani2019introduction,obertelli2021modern}.\\

\subsection{The Glauber model}\label{sec:glauber}
The Glauber Model is a theoretical framework widely employed in high-energy nuclear and particle physics to describe elastic and inelastic scattering processes involving composite targets, such as nuclei. Originally formulated by Roy J.~Glauber, the model provides a quantum-mechanical description of multiple scattering in high-energy collisions and is particularly applicable in the regime where the de Broglie wavelength of the projectile is much smaller than the size of the target.\\
The most general implementations of the Glauber Model incorporate the following key components:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Eikonal Approximation:} In the high-energy limit, the projectile is assumed to traverse the target along a straight-line trajectory, experiencing only small-angle deflections. This simplifies the scattering problem by reducing it to a phase-shift analysis of the incident wavefunction. A formal introduction to the eikonal approximation is provided in Subsection~\ref{sec:eol}.
    \item \textbf{Multiple Scattering Formalism:} The total scattering amplitude is constructed by summing over all possible interactions between the projectile and the individual nucleons of the target. This involves integrating over the spatial distributions of the nucleons, which becomes computationally demanding for large nuclei. To address this, further simplifications are applied, leading to the so-called \textit{optical-limit approximation}, discussed in Subsection~\ref{sec:eol}.
    \item \textbf{Impact Parameter Formalism:} The scattering cross section is expressed as an integral over the transverse distance (impact parameter) between the centers of the projectile and the target nucleus (see Fig.~\ref{fig:impact_par_glauber}). This semi-classical treatment enables a geometric interpretation of the scattering process and facilitates a probabilistic formulation of nuclear interactions.
\end{itemize}

Two principal approaches are commonly used to derive cross sections within the Glauber framework:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Probability Approach (PA):} In this semiclassical method, nucleon-nucleon interactions are treated as independent binary collisions. All combinatorial configurations of these interactions are considered, and the total reaction cross section is obtained by integrating over all possible impact parameters (see Fig.~\ref{fig:impact_par_glauber}). In this picture, nucleons are often approximated as \textit{billiard balls}~\cite{bertulani2019introduction}. The \textit{abrasion-ablation model}~\cite{hufner1975abrasion}, which can be extended to incorporate isospin-dependent nucleon-nucleon cross sections, is a widely used application of the probability approach, for instance in \textsc{Geant4} simulations~\cite{agostinelli2003geant4}. A comprehensive overview of the PA is provided in~\cite{schindler2017total} and more details in~\cite{bertulani2019introduction}.
    \item \textbf{Eikonal Optical Limit (EOL):} The present work focuses on the quantum-mechanical formulation known as the EOL approach. It is based on the time-independent Schr\"odinger equation, incorporating the eikonal wave approximation and the introduction of a complex optical potential. This results in a compact and analytically tractable expression for the scattering amplitude, making it particularly suitable for high-energy collisions involving extended targets.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Eikonal optical-limit approximation (EOL)}\label{sec:eol}
The starting point for this discussion is the elastic scattering process, in which both the projectile and the target remain in their ground states, and no nucleons are removed from either system.
A good description of the elastic scattering process allows us to later derive the reaction and the total cross section.\\
Beginning with the quantum mechanical view from the time-independent Schr\"odinger equation as stationary treatment of the elastic scattering process
\begin{equation}\label{eq:schroedinger}
H\psi_{\vec{k}}(\vec{r}) = E\psi_{\vec{k}}(\vec{r}) \, ,
\end{equation}
given the Hamiltonian
\begin{equation}\label{eq:hamiltonian}
H = -\frac{\hbar^2}{2\mu} \vec{\nabla}^2 + V(\vec{r}) \, ,
\end{equation}
where $\mu = \frac{m_T \cdot m_P}{m_T+m_P}$ is the  reduced mass of the system, composed of the target nucleus with mass $m_T$ and the projectile with mass $m_P$ .
The objective is to determine  the wavefunction $\psi_{\vec{k}}(\vec{r})$ that satisfies Eq.~\ref{eq:schroedinger}. This wavefunction consists of the sum of the incoming plane wave $\varphi_{\vec{k}}(\vec{r})$ and the outgoing scattered spherical wave $\psi^{sc}_{\vec{k}}(\vec{r})$, i.e.
\begin{equation}\label{eq:tot_wave}
\psi_{\vec{k}}(\vec{r}) = A [e^{ikr} + f(\theta) \frac{e^{ikr}}{r}] \equiv \varphi_{\vec{k}}(\vec{r}) + \psi_{\vec{k}}(\vec{r}) \, .
\end{equation}
The factor $f(\theta)$ in front of the outgoing spherical wave is called scattering amplitude. Assuming a spherical central potential the scattering amplitude depends on the polar angle $\theta$ only, the azimuthal angle $\varphi$ does not have to be considered. 
The integral representation of the Schr\"odinger equation with the implementation of Green's operator G(E) is given by
$$
\left| \psi_{\vec{k}} \right\rangle = \left| \phi_{\vec{k}} \right\rangle + G_0(E)V(\vec{r}) \left| \phi_{\vec{k}} \right\rangle,
$$
and following the derivation in Section 3.3 of Ref.~\cite{bertulani2019introduction} leads to following solution of the wavefunction
\begin{equation}
\psi_{\vec{k}}(\vec{r}) = \frac{1}{(2\pi)^{3/2}} \left[ e^{i\vec{k}\cdot \vec{r}} + \frac{e^{ikr}}{r} \left( -2\pi^2 \frac{2\mu}{\hbar^2} \right) \langle \varphi_{\vec{k}'} | V(\vec{r}) | \psi_{\vec{k}} \rangle \right] \, ,
\end{equation}
which gives for the scattering amplitude $f(\theta)$
\begin{equation}\label{eq:sc_amp_pot}
\begin{split}
f(\theta) & = -2\pi^2 \left( \frac{2\mu}{\hbar^2} \right) \langle \varphi_{\vec{k}'} | V(\vec{r}) | \psi_{\vec{k}} \rangle \\
 & = -2\pi^2 \left( \frac{2\mu}{\hbar^2} \right) \int \varphi_{\vec{k}'}^*(\vec{r}) V(\vec{r}) \psi_{\vec{k}}(\vec{r}) d\vec{r} \, ,
\end{split}
\end{equation}
with $\varphi_{\vec{k}'}$ the incoming wave and $\psi_{\vec{k}}$ the outgoing spherical wave accordingly, as in Eq.~\ref{eq:tot_wave}.\\
The (differential) elastic scattering cross section can be derived from the scattering amplitude $f(\theta)$ via the conservation law of particle current\footnote{A derivation of this equation can be found in~\cite{mayer2013kernphysik}, Section 4.2}
\begin{equation}\label{eq:el_scatt_cs}
\left( \frac{\mathrm{d} \sigma_{el}}{\mathrm{d} \Omega} \right) = |f(\theta)|^2 \qquad \Rightarrow \qquad \sigma_{el} = \int \mathrm{d} \Omega \, |f(\theta)|^2 \, .
\end{equation}

\subsubsection{Eikonal Wave Approximation}
The eikonal wave approximation can be applied when the incident particle's kinetic energy is much larger than the potential's strength ($ E >> V$) and the potential should vary slowly over the wavelength of the particle. This approximation is most accurate for scattering at small angles. Are those prerequisites given, then the eikonal wave approximation assumes that the scattered particle follows a nearly straight-line trajectory (like a classical particle). The wavefunction is approximated as a plane wave modulated by a phase factor. The term "eikonal" comes from the Greek word for "image" or "likeness," reflecting its origin in optics (ray optics).
Following the derivation of Ref.~\cite{bertulani2019introduction} the solution for the wave function in Eq.~\ref{eq:schroedinger} is expressed as
\begin{equation}\label{eq:wave_sol}
\psi_{\mathbf{k}}(\mathbf{r}) = e^{ikz} \varphi(\mathbf{c}, z) \, ,
\end{equation}
where $e^{ikz}$ is the plane wave in $z$ direction, $\varphi(\mathbf{c}, z)$ the distortion caused by the potential and $\mathbf{c}$ the space vector.
Inserting the plane wave function from Eq.~\ref{eq:wave_sol} in the Schr\"odinger equation~\ref{eq:schroedinger} and omitting higher order derivative terms, due to the slowly varying potential over the particle's wave length (full derivation can be found in~\cite{bertulani2019introduction}) the distortion $\varphi(\mathbf{c}, z)$ can be simplified by
\begin{equation}
\varphi(\mathbf{c}, z) = \exp \left\{ -\frac{i}{\hbar \nu} \int_{-\infty}^{z} V(\mathbf{c}, z') \, dz' \right\} \, ,
\end{equation}
where $\mathbf{c}$ is the space vector and $z$ the coordinate along the beam direction.  
This is the aforementioned the phase factor, originating from the distortion due to the potential, called eikonal phase
\begin{equation}\label{eq:eikonal_phase}
\chi(\vec{c}, z) = - \frac{1}{\hbar v} \int_{-\infty}^{z} V(\vec{c}, z') dz' \, .
\end{equation}
Summarizing the eikonal wave function from Eq.~\ref{eq:wave_sol}
\begin{equation}
\psi_k(\vec{r}) = e^{ikz+i\chi(\vec{c},z)} \, .
\end{equation}
Upon simplification, see Section 8.6 in Ref.~\cite{bertulani2019introduction} we get for the scattering amplitude
\begin{equation}\label{eq:final_sc_amp}
f(\theta) = -\frac{ik}{2\pi} \int \mathrm{d} \vec{c} \, e^{i \vec{q} \cdot \vec{c}} \left( e^{i \chi(\vec{c})} - 1 \right) \, ,
\end{equation}
and inserting this in Eq.~\ref{eq:el_scatt_cs} and following further simplification we finally get for the elastic cross section $\sigma_{el}$
\begin{equation}
\sigma_{el} = \int \left| e^{i \chi(\vec{c})} - 1 \right|^2 \, \mathrm{d} \vec{c} \, .
\end{equation}
Without derivation we present here the \textbf{optical theorem} which relates the scattering amplitude $f(\theta)$ at $\theta = 0$ to the total cross section (see Eq.~\ref{eq:tot_cs_def})
\begin{equation}
\sigma_T = \frac{4\pi}{k} \, \text{Im} \left[ f(\theta = 0) \right] \, .
\end{equation}
The optical theorem, formulated in the context of optics -- specifically for the scattering and absorption of light -- was later extended and applied to other wave phenomena, including quantum mechanical scattering of particles.\\
The name stems from the fact that $\text{Im}\, f(0)$ is related to the imaginary part of the refractive index $n$, which the scattering potential has for the incident wave. We have $\text{Im}\, f(0) = \frac{k^2}{2\pi} \text{Im}\, n$. The imaginary part of the refractive index describes the absorption of the beam. The optical theorem is therefore an an expression of the relationship between absorption in the forward direction and the total cross section~\cite{mayer2013kernphysik}.
The optical theorem can be derived using the fundamental principle of particle current conservation, see Section 6.4.3 in~\cite{sakurai2020modern}.\\
From Eq.~\ref{eq:tot_cs_def}, the scattering amplitude (Eq.~\ref{eq:final_sc_amp}) and the aforementioned optical theorem relation we can summarize
\begin{align}
\sigma_{T} &=  2 \int \left(1 - \text{Re}\left[e^{i\chi(\vec{c})}\right]\right) d\vec{c} \, , \\
\sigma_{R} &= \sigma_{T} - \sigma{el} \\
           &= \int \left(1 - \left|e^{i\chi(\vec{c})}\right|^2\right) d\vec{c} \label{eq:simga_reaction} \, .
\end{align}

\subsubsection{Optical potential and optical limit}

In the following, we introduce the concept of the optical potential \( V(\vec{r}) \), as it appears in the Hamiltonian. 
A rigorous theoretical understanding of this potential would ideally come from a derivation based on nucleon-nucleon (NN) interactions using a fully self-consistent many-body framework. 
However, such an \textit{ab initio} treatment remains highly complex and has only been partially addressed in the literature. 
Therefore, in most practical applications, the optical potential is introduced phenomenologically, with its parameters fitted to elastic and inelastic scattering cross section data.

There are two main approaches to construct the optical potential:

\paragraph{1. Phenomenological Optical Potential}

This approach uses an analogy to optics, where a complex index of refraction models both wave transmission and absorption. 
Similarly, the nuclear optical potential is written as
\begin{equation}
V(r) = U(r) + iW(r) \, .
\end{equation}
Here, the real part \( U(r) \) typically takes the form of a Woods-Saxon potential and governs elastic scattering. 
The imaginary part \( W(r) \), responsible for absorption into non-elastic channels, is more difficult to model and is usually decomposed into volume, surface, and mixed components. 
Detailed models can be found in Ref.~\cite{obertelli2021modern} and Ref.~\cite{mayer2013kernphysik}.

\paragraph{2. Microscopic Optical Potential and the Optical Limit}

This approach starts from the interaction of a projectile nucleon with individual nucleons in the target. 
The first-order optical potential is given by
\begin{equation}
V_{\text{opt}}(\vec{r}) = t_{pn} \, \rho_n(\vec{r}) + t_{pp} \, \rho_p(\vec{r}) \, .
\end{equation}
Here, \( t_{pn} \) and \( t_{pp} \) are the transition matrix elements for proton-neutron and proton-proton interactions, and \( \rho_n(\vec{r}) \), \( \rho_p(\vec{r}) \) are the local neutron and proton densities in the target. 
These elements are related to the scattering amplitude by
\begin{equation}
t_{pi} = \langle \varphi_{\vec{k}'} | V(\vec{r}) | \psi_{\vec{k}} \rangle = -\frac{\hbar^2}{2\mu} \cdot \frac{1}{2\pi^2} f_{pi}(\theta), \quad \text{with } i = \text{proton, neutron} \, .
\end{equation}
To extend this to nucleus-nucleus scattering, the transition elements are often averaged over isospin, yielding an effective nucleon-nucleon matrix element
\begin{equation}
t_{NN} \approx -i \, \frac{\hbar \nu}{2} \, \sigma_{NN} \, .
\end{equation}
Taking into account the spatial distributions of nucleons in both the projectile and target nuclei, the point-like approximation must be replaced by an integral over their respective density profiles
\begin{equation}
V_{\text{OL}}(\vec{b}) = -i \, \frac{\hbar \nu}{2} \, \sigma_{NN} \int \mathrm{d}^3 r \, \rho_P(\vec{r}) \, \rho_T(\vec{r} - \vec{b}) \, .
\end{equation}
In this formulation, \( \vec{b} \) denotes the impact parameter, i.e., the transverse displacement between the centers of the projectile and target nuclei. 
The resulting potential is often referred to as a \textit{double-folded optical potential} (see Ref.~\cite{obertelli2021modern}, Sec. 8.2.7).
Substituting this potential into the eikonal phase expression, the total reaction cross section becomes
\begin{equation}\label{eq:final_react_cs}
\sigma_R = 2\pi \int \mathrm{d} \vec{b} \left[ 1 - \exp \left( -\sigma_{NN} \int \mathrm{d} z' \int \mathrm{d}^3 r \, \rho_P(\vec{r}) \, \rho_T(\vec{r} - \vec{b}) \right) \right] \, .
\end{equation}
For completeness, we also include the expression for the \textit{charge-changing} cross section \( \sigma_{\Delta Z} \), which is of central importance to this work (see Section~\ref{subsec:cc_cs}). 
In this case, the reaction is sensitive only to the removal of one or more protons from the projectile. 
Accordingly, the full projectile density \( \rho_P(\vec{r}) \) is replaced by the proton density distribution \( \rho_P^p(\vec{r}) \)
\begin{equation}\label{eq:final_cc_formula}
\sigma_{\Delta Z} = 2\pi \int \mathrm{d} \vec{b} \left[ 1 - \exp \left( -\sigma_{NN} \int \mathrm{d} z' \int \mathrm{d}^3 r \, \rho_P^p(\vec{r}) \, \rho_T(\vec{r} - \vec{b}) \right) \right] \, .
\end{equation}
This expression differs from the total reaction cross section only by this substitution, which reflects the fact that the observable is sensitive specifically to proton-removal processes.


\subsection{Input to the Glauber Model}

As shown in Eq.~\ref{eq:final_react_cs}, the total reaction cross section depends only on the density distributions of the target and projectile, as well as on the isospin-averaged free nucleon-nucleon cross section, $\sigma_{NN}$.
\begin{description}

\item[Nucleon-Nucleon Cross Section]
The isospin-averaged nucleon-nucleon cross section $\sigma_{NN}$ is computed based on the number of protons ($Z_P$, $Z_T$) and neutrons ($N_P$, $N_T$) in the projectile and target nuclei
\begin{equation}
\sigma_{NN} = \frac{N_P N_T \sigma_{nn} + Z_P Z_T \sigma_{pp} + (Z_P N_T + N_P Z_T) \sigma_{np}}{A_P A_T} \, .
\end{equation}
Here, $\sigma_{nn}$, $\sigma_{pp}$, and $\sigma_{np}$ denote the neutron-neutron, proton-proton, and neutron-proton cross sections, respectively. Fig.~\ref{fig:nn_lukas} shows the corresponding experimental nucleon-nucleon cross section data, with $\sigma_{pp}$ plotted in red and $\sigma_{np}$ in black. The Coulomb contribution is omitted.
At low energies, the effective $\sigma_{pp}$ cross section is reduced relative to $\sigma_{np}$, primarily due to Coulomb repulsion in the pp system. Additionally, neutron-proton scattering benefits from stronger interaction channels due to isospin coupling, which enhances the total cross section. At high energies, the distinction vanishes, and one can approximate $\sigma_{pp} = \sigma_{nn} = \sigma_{np}$.

The second essential input to the Glauber model is the nuclear density distribution of the projectile, \( \rho_P(\vec{r}) \), and the target, \( \rho_T(\vec{r}) \). The dependence of the nuclear radius on the mass number \( A \) was first established in early Rutherford scattering experiments, which demonstrated that it scales in first order as
\begin{equation}
R = r_0 A^{1/3} \, ,
\end{equation}
where \( r_0 = (1.3 \pm 0.1)\,\text{fm} \), as reported in~\cite{pollard1935nuclear}. These experiments probe the distribution of protons via their electric charge. Assuming that protons and neutrons share an identical spatial distribution, the measured charge density serves as a valid proxy for the total nuclear density. This approximation holds well for stable nuclei such as \( ^{12}\text{C} \), which is investigated in this work.
Nuclear charge radii are determined experimentally through high-energy electron scattering or through spectroscopy of muonic atoms. In the case of electron scattering, the differential cross section for an extended target such as a nucleus is expressed as
\begin{equation}
\left( \frac{d\sigma}{d\Omega} \right)_{\text{exp}} = \left( \frac{d\sigma}{d\Omega} \right)_{\text{Mott}} \cdot \left| F(q^2) \right|^2 \, ,
\end{equation}
where \( F(q^2) \) is the nuclear form factor and accounts for the finite size and internal structure of the nucleus. The Mott cross section includes relativistic and spin corrections to the Rutherford cross section\footnote{The asterisk denotes that the nuclear recoil is neglected in the given expression for electron-nucleus scattering.}
\begin{equation}
\left( \frac{d\sigma}{d\Omega} \right)^{*}_{\text{Mott}} = \left( \frac{d\sigma}{d\Omega} \right)_{\text{Rutherford}} \cdot \left( 1 - \beta^2 \sin^2 \frac{\theta}{2} \right), \quad \beta = \frac{v}{c} \, .
\end{equation}
The form factor \( F(\mathbf{q}^2) \) is the Fourier transform of the spatial charge distribution
\begin{equation}
F(\mathbf{q}^2) = \int e^{i\mathbf{q}\cdot\mathbf{x}/\hbar} \rho(\mathbf{x}) \,\mathrm{d}^3x 
\quad \Rightarrow \quad 
\rho(r) = \frac{1}{(2\pi)^3} \int F(\mathbf{q}^2) e^{-i\mathbf{q}\cdot\mathbf{x}/\hbar} \,\mathrm{d}^3q \, .
\end{equation}
Thus, by measuring the differential cross section and comparing it to the known Mott cross section, the form factor can be extracted. An inverse Fourier transform then allows the reconstruction of the nuclear charge density. In practice, however, the accessible range of momentum transfer \( \mathbf{q} \) is limited.
A common approach is therefore to assume a parameterized functional form for \( \rho(r) \), and fit its parameters to best reproduce the experimental form factor. One frequently used choice is the Fermi distribution; see Section 2.1 of Ref.~\cite{mayer2013kernphysik}.
An alternative phenomenological method is the Energy Density Functional (EDF) theory. In this approach, the nuclear Hamiltonian is expressed as a functional of the local one-body density. The parameters of this functional are determined by fitting to experimental observables, such as charge and matter radii. EDF methods are not explicitly based on nucleon-nucleon interactions but enable the derivation of nuclear density profiles and extrapolations to exotic nuclei. A detailed discussion of EDF can be found in Section 3.6 of Ref.~\cite{obertelli2021modern}.

\end{description}

\item[Pauli Blocking]
The Pauli exclusion principle, formulated by Wolfgang Pauli, prohibits identical fermions -- such as protons or neutrons -- from occupying the same quantum state. In the context of nuclear reactions, this principle implies that not all final states in inelastic collisions are accessible, particularly those involving nuclear excitation. Consequently, the available phase space is reduced, leading to a suppression of the total reaction cross section.
This phenomenon, referred to as \textit{Pauli blocking}, becomes especially relevant at low incident energies. A detailed discussion can be found in Refs.~\cite{hussein1991microscopic, bertulani1986heavy, bertulani2001density, bertulani2010pauli}. A quantitative expression for the Pauli-blocked in-medium nucleon-nucleon cross section is given in Ref.~\cite{teixeira2022nuclear} as
\begin{equation}\label{eq:pauli_blocked_cs}
\sigma_{NN}^{\text{Pauli}}(E, \rho_P, \rho_T) = \sigma_{NN}^{\text{free}}(E)
\cdot \frac{1}{1 + 1.892 \left( \frac{|\rho_P - \rho_T|}{\tilde{\rho} \rho_0} \right)^{2.75}}
\cdot
\begin{cases}
1 - \frac{37.02 \tilde{\rho}^{2/3}}{E} \, , & \text{if } E > 46.27 \tilde{\rho}^{2/3}, \\
\frac{E}{231.38 \tilde{\rho}^{2/3}} \, , & \text{if } E \leq 46.27 \tilde{\rho}^{2/3},
\end{cases}
\end{equation}
with
\[
\tilde{\rho} = \frac{\rho_P + \rho_T}{\rho_0}, \quad \rho_0 = 0.16\,\mathrm{fm}^{-3} \, .
\]
To incorporate Pauli blocking into the Glauber model, the free-space nucleon-nucleon cross section $\sigma_{NN}^{\text{free}}$ must be replaced with the Pauli-suppressed cross section $\sigma_{NN}^{\text{Pauli}}$ in both cross section formulae Eq.~\ref{eq:final_react_cs} and Eq.~\ref{eq:final_cc_formula}. 
\item[Coulomb Repulsion]
For systems in which both the projectile and the target carry positive charge, such as in $^{12}$C + $^{12}$C collisions, Coulomb repulsion plays a significant role. The eikonal approximation assumes straight-line trajectories, which becomes invalid as the Coulomb force deflects the incoming projectile. This deflection reduces the probability of the nuclei reaching distances where strong nuclear interactions dominate, effectively suppressing the interaction cross section -- particularly at low beam energies where the kinetic energy is comparable to the Coulomb barrier.
In the non-relativistic case, the distance of closest approach in a head-on collision can be calculated by setting the kinetic energy of the projectile equal to the repulsive Coulomb potential
\begin{equation}
\frac{1}{2}mv^{2} = \frac{Z_T Z_P e^{2}}{r} \quad \Rightarrow \quad r_{\text{min}} = \frac{2 Z_T Z_P e^{2}}{m v^2} \, .
\end{equation}
In the relativistic center-of-mass frame, this becomes
\begin{equation}
r_{\text{min}} = \frac{Z_T Z_P e^{2}}{\gamma \mu v^2} \, ,
\end{equation}
with $\gamma$ being the Lorentz factor and $\mu$ the reduced mass of the system.
The corrected impact parameter is then given as
\begin{equation}
b' = a_0 + \sqrt{a_{0}^2 + b^2} \, ,
\end{equation}
where $a_0 = \frac{r_{min}}{2}$ is the half distance of closest approach. For more details see Ref.~\cite{teixeira2022nuclear}.
As a consequence, the lower limit of the integration over the impact parameter in the total reaction and charge changing cross section (Eq.~\ref{eq:final_react_cs} and Eq.~\ref{eq:final_cc_formula}) is modified from $b=0$ to $b'= r_{\text{min}}$.

\item[Fermi Motion]
In the basic Glauber model, nucleons are assumed to be at rest within the nuclei. However, nucleons in a bound system exhibit intrinsic motion. In the ground state, momenta up to the Fermi momentum ($p_F \approx 250\,\mathrm{MeV}/c$) are possible for medium to heavy nuclei. This motion alters the effective nucleon-nucleon collision energy and can, in principle, influence the reaction cross section.
Ref.~\cite{teixeira2022nuclear} investigates this effect and finds that the correction to the total reaction cross section remains below 0.1\% for beam energies above 200\,AMeV. Therefore, Fermi motion can be neglected in the high-energy context of this work.
However, in quasi-free scattering reactions, the internal momentum distribution of nucleons plays a significant role. This aspect will be discussed in the following section.

\end{description}

\subsection{Quasi-Free Scattering (QFS) Reactions}\label{sec:qfs_theo}
Quasi-free scattering (QFS) reactions, as a subset of direct reactions, are processes where a projectile nucleon interacts with a target nucleon via the strong nuclear force in a single and fast localized event at large momentum transfer which allows for a highly localized vertex. The relative kinetic energies of the participants, typically on the order of  $E_{kin}\gtrsim$ 100 AMeV, are high\footnote{Which is needed for the single nucleon-nucleon interaction. The \textit{de Broglie wavelength} of a 100 MeV proton corresponds to $\lambda \approx 2.79fm$ which is in the order of the proton diameter ($\approx 1.6 fm$).}.\newline
In QFS experiments conducted in direct kinematics, a proton serves as the projectile, colliding with a nucleon or a cluster within the target nucleus. Conversely, in inverse kinematics, the nucleus of interest becomes the projectile, while a proton or proton-like particle is used as the target. Despite these differing setups, both approaches are fundamentally equivalent, differing only due to a Lorentz transformation of the reference frame.\newline
As the name implies, QFS reactions are conceptually similar to free nucleon-nucleon scattering, with the primary approximation being that the influence of the residual nucleus is neglected to first order. This approximation simplifies the theoretical description of the process, allowing to treat the interaction as a two-body problem within a (only weakly interfering) nuclear environment.\newline
The first experiments confirming the existence of quasi-free scattering processes were conducted at the University of California, Berkeley, in 1952 by O. Chamberlain and Emilio Segr\`e~\cite{chamberlain1952proton}. In their study, lithium nuclei were bombarded with 350 MeV protons, and coincident proton pairs were observed with an opening angle of approximately 90$^{\circ}$. That same year, J.B. Cladis, W.N. Hess, and B.J. Moyer published results on the scattering of 340 MeV protons on deuterium and carbon targets~\cite{cladis1952nucleon}, further substantiating the phenomenon.\newline
In 1957, Tyr\'en, Maris, and Hillman designed an experiment aimed at fully characterizing proton-proton collisions within the quasi-free scattering framework~\cite{MARIS19581}. Their results validated the assumption of a direct and clean interaction between the projectile and the target nucleon, free from significant distortions caused by the surrounding nucleus. Furthermore, these experiments demonstrated that QFS reactions could serve as a powerful tool for probing nuclear structure and testing predictions of the shell model. Specifically, they enabled the study of key nuclear parameters such as spin-orbit splitting and energy differences between nuclear shells probing the shell evolution. \newline
In QFS reactions, these parameters can be extracted by analyzing the reaction products, which include the two correlated outgoing protons, the residual nucleus, and any gamma rays emitted during de-excitation of the residual nucleus. The detailed measurements of these observables provide critical insights into the underlying nuclear structure and dynamics.\newline
The experimental discoveries and theoretical insights from these early studies strongly pushed the advancements in the theoretical modeling of QFS reactions. These models have since become essential tools for understanding nucleon-nucleon interactions within the nuclear medium and for further investigations of nuclear structure and reaction mechanisms.\newline
\subsubsection{Kinematics of QFS Reactions}\label{sec:kin_qfs}
A simplified picture, which however explains the essential kinematics of the QFS reaction, can be found in Fig.~\ref{fig:sketch_qfs}: we have an incoming proton knocking out a nuclear constituent (proton/neutron) in a quasi-free nucleon-nucleon collision ending with a final state having the scattered proton, the scattered  off nuclear constituent and the rest nucleus (A-1), which could be either in the ground-state or an excited state. In this picture index 0 is assigned to the incoming proton, 1 to the knocked out nucleon, 2 to the outgoing projectile proton, A to the initial nucleus and A-1 to the final nucleus accordingly.
\begin{figure}[htpb]
    \centering
    %\includegraphics[width=0.95\textwidth,keepaspectratio=true]{Figures/sketch_qfs.pdf}
    \includegraphics[width=\textwidth,keepaspectratio=true]{Figures/sketch_qfs.png}
    \caption{
   	Simplified picture of the QFS reaction process in direct kinematics.  
    }
    \label{fig:sketch_qfs}
\end{figure}
As from standard textbooks (see~\cite[Chapter~6.2]{povh2013teilchen}) the energy-momentum conservation of the reaction can be expressed as
\begin{equation}\label{eq:four_mom_cons_qfs}
P_A  +  P_0 = P_1 + P_2 +P_{A-1} \, , 
\end{equation}
with $P_i$ the four momentum $(E_i,\mathbf{p_i})$.\newline
In direct kinematics as presented in Fig.~\ref{fig:sketch_qfs} the separation energy  of the ejected nucleon for a certain final state of the nucleus A-1 is given by\footnote{In inverse kinematics the four-momentum vectors need to be boosted to the center of mass frame of the initial nucleus via Lorentz transformation.}
\begin{equation}
S = T_0 -(T_1+T_2 +T_{A-1}); \; \text{with $T_i$ the kinetic energy of particle $i$} \, .
\label{eq:sep_e}
\end{equation}
In the idealized shell model the separation energy equals to the (negative) energy of the nucleus' single-particle state. In the case where the final nucleus (A-1) (corresponding to a one-proton knockout) remains in its ground state, its total energy in the center-of-mass frame of the initial nucleus is given by
\begin{equation}
E_{A-1} = M_{A-1}c^{2} + T_{A-1} \, ,
\end{equation}
where \( M_{A-1} \) denotes the rest mass of the final nucleus and \( T_{A-1} \) its kinetic energy after the quasi-free nucleon-nucleon collision.\newline
If a nucleon has been ejected from an inner shell resulting in a hole state, the final nucleus will be in an excited state
\begin{equation}
E^{*}_{A-1} =  M_{A-1}c^2  +T_{A-1} + E_{exe} \, .
\end{equation} 
From the experimental point of view, $E_{exe}$ is directly accessible via gamma detection from the transition of the final nucleus from the excited to the ground state or via the detection of evaporated neutrons from higher excited states.\newline
From momentum conservation follows that in the center-of-mass frame of the initial nucleus ($\mathbf{p_A} = 0$), the recoil momentum of the nucleus in final state $\mathbf{p_{A-1}}$ equals to $-\mathbf{p_i}$, the momentum of the initial nucleon inside the nucleus A pointing in opposite direction. \newline
In addition the four-momentum of the inner nucleon can be deduced from momentum measurement of the initial and final state free nucleons
\begin{equation}\label{eq:miss_mom}
P_i \approx P_{miss} \equiv P_1 + P_2  - P_0 \, ,
\end{equation}
where $P_{miss}$ is the so called "measured missing four-momentum of the reaction"~\cite{patsyuk2021unperturbed}\footnote{$P_{miss}$ is only equal to $P_i$ for the unperturbed QFS (no ISI/FSI) case.}.
Thereupon, the separation energy measurement and the recoil momentum distribution fully describe the single particle state in the various shell levels. 
In addition and as complementary method $\gamma$ rays can be measured in coincidence with the reaction and consequently exclusive cross section and momentum distribution measurements of the single particle states are accessible.\newline
Considering the removal of a single nucleon from an initial nucleus state $\Psi^A_i$ with A nucleons and initial spin \textit{I} and final nucleus state  $\Psi^{A-1}_f$ with final spin $I_f$ an overlap function between initial and final state many-body wave function can be written as
\begin{equation}
\langle \vec{r}, \Psi_f^{A^{-1}} | \Psi_i^A \rangle = \sum_j C_j^{if} \psi_j(\vec{r}), \quad with  |I - I_f| < j < |I + I_f| \, ,
\end{equation}
where $S_j^{if} = |C_j^{if}|^2$ is the commonly named spectroscopic factor, see Ref.~\cite[Sec.2.1]{hansen2003direct}. $S_j^{if}$ is summed over all final single particle states m (from $-j$ to $j$). It is unity for nucleon removal from a pure single particle state and in this picture equals (2j+1) when the nucleon was removed from a filled j-subshell.
In realistic phenomenological models the spectroscopic factor is linked to the exclusive experimental cross section measurement of a single particle state $\sigma_{sp}(nlj)$ and the theoretical predicted one, as in Ref.~\cite{hansen2003direct}
\begin{equation}
\sigma_{th}^{if} = \sum_j S_j^{if}\,\sigma_{sp}(nlj) \, ,
\end{equation}
where $\sigma_{sp}$ are the theoretical cross sections for the normalized wave functions $\Psi_j$ of the final state nucleus A-1 with the appropriate quantum numbers.\newline
From this considerations the spectroscopic factor can be used to probe the theoretical shell predictions. In past this was already done with direct QFS-reactions $(e,e'p)$. Results for the spectroscopic factor with data obtained at the NIKHEF facility are shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:spec_fac}. The substantial reduction of the spectroscopic factor with respect to the independent particle model (IPM) or mean field of $\approx 35\%$ indicates a substantial depletion of the single-particle states and inferring from this a refined model prediction has to be applied\footnote{The mean field potential does not consider spin-spin interaction $V_{ss}$, non central tensor-potential $V_T$ or spin-orbit interaction $V_{LS}$.}.\newline
\begin{figure}[h!]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.65\textwidth,keepaspectratio=true]{Figures/spec_factor.png}
    \caption{Normalized spectroscopic factors from the $(e,e'p)$ reaction as a function of target mass adopted from~\cite{DICKHOFF2004377}. As input for the theoretical cross sections the prediction from mean field models were used. }
    \label{fig:spec_fac}
\end{figure}
While QFS-reaction with electrons $(e,e'p)$ in direct kinematics is a valuable method to make precise measurements for stable (target) nuclei it is not suitable for the experimental analysis with exotic (neutron or proton rich) nuclei. Due to their short lifetime (e.g. $^{52}Ca$ with $\tau = 4.6s$) they can hardly serve as a target. Inducing the reaction in inverse kinematics - having electrons as targets and the exotic nuclei of interest as projectiles - is also not feasible since no free electrons can be captured as target and even more important the required center of mass energies in such an asymmetric system would require extremely high beam energies. The alternative approach is to use proton induced quasi-free scattering in inverse kinematics where the exotic beam impinges on an extended proton target such as liquid hydrogen $H_2$  or a fixed proton rich target such as $CH_2$\footnote{Herefore a carbon target reference run is used to extract the QFS-reaction events from the $CH_2$ target run.}.\newline
Summarizing, the QFS-reaction technique in inverse kinematics with exotic nuclei as in-flight projectiles and a proton-like target opens new possibilities to probe theoretical model predictions of exotic nuclei far off stability which haven't been accessible before.\newline
For precise cross section measurements of the single particle states $\sigma_{sp}$ the kinematical characteristics of the QFS-reaction products have to be considered for correct identification of QFS-events and clear background subtraction. The following descriptions are already implicitly embedded within Eq.~\ref{eq:four_mom_cons_qfs}.\newline
As starting point one compares the QFS-reaction of the two nucleons with the two dimensional non-central collision of free point-like particles in non-relativistic kinematics. Since both kinetic energy and momenta are conserved a  clear signature is expected: the opening angle of the scattered particles -- assuming they have the same rest mass -- is exactly 90$^{\circ}$. 
However, at kinetic energies of 400 AMeV and more, relativistic effects affect the opening angle of the scattered particles\footnote{Relativistic considerations become relevant at velocities $ \beta \gtrapprox 0.1 c$. This corresponds to $\approx$ 4.5 MeV.}, see Fig.~\ref{fig:correlation_mrunmoy}. 
\begin{figure}[h!]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\textwidth,height=12cm,keepaspectratio=true]{Figures/mrunmoy_correlation.pdf}
    \caption{Kinematical correlations between the polar scattering angles ($\theta_1$,$\theta_2$) of two protons undergoing elastic scattering, shown for beam energies of 10, 100, 300, 500, 700, 900, and 1000 MeV. The uppermost straight line corresponds to a beam energy of 10 MeV, where the opening angle between the two protons is approximately $90^{\circ}$, as expected in the non-relativistic regime. As the beam energy increases, relativistic effects cause a pronounced bending of the correlation curves. Courtesy: \textit{Mrunmoy Jena}.}
    \label{fig:correlation_mrunmoy}
\end{figure}
For the final description of the process it has to be considered that the projectile nucleon inside the nucleus has a non negligible momentum (see $P_i$ in Eq.~\ref{eq:miss_mom})  and in addition the separation energy $S_{p/n}$ has to be expended to remove the scattered nucleon of the nuclear potential of the projectile nucleus.\newline
To account for the nucleon momentum the picture of the Fermi gas model where protons and neutrons freely move inside the nucleus' potential is applied. In the ground state of the nucleus the nucleons can reach a momentum up to the Fermi momentum $p_F$. Except for the light nuclei, the Fermi momentum is almost independent of A and amounts to $ p_f \approx 250$ MeV/c \footnote{For light nuclei like $ ^{12}C$, $p_F \approx 230$ MeV/c can be assumed~\cite{moniz1971nuclear}}. The mean quadratic momenta of the nucleons is related to the Fermi momentum by
\begin{equation}
\langle \mathbf{p}^2 \rangle = \frac{3}{5}p^2_F \footnote{From the derivation of the mean kinetic energy of the nucleons $\langle E_{\text{kin}} \rangle = \frac{\int_0^{p_F} E_{\text{kin}} \, p^2 \, dp}{\int_0^{p_F} p^2 \, dp} = \frac{3}{5} \cdot \frac{p_F^2}{2M}$} \, .
\end{equation}
The width of the momentum distribution of the nucleons is given in the Goldhaber model by
\begin{equation}
\sigma^2 = \sigma_0^2 K(A-K)/(A-1), \, \text{with} \, \sigma_0 \approx 90\,\mathrm{MeV/c}\, ,
\end{equation}
where A is the mass number of the projectile nucleus and K the mass number of the fragment after scattering. For a detailed derivation and further readings see Ref.~\cite{goldhaber1974statistical} and~\cite{FESHBACH1973300}.
In the impulse approximation, where we assume that the interaction between the projectile and target nucleons has approximately the same form as the interaction between two nucleons in free space, the inner momenta of the scattered nucleons smear out the angular correlations of the outgoing fragment -- which has the same momentum as the knocked out nucleon in the cms system of the nucleus but points in opposite direction -- as well as of the two nucleons involved in the scattering. As consequence the opening angle distribution of the two scattered nucleons gets broadened as well as the azimuthal angular correlation which for the assumption of zero nucleon momentum sharply peaked at $\Delta\varphi = 180^{\circ}$.\newline
In view of the added nucleon momentum the kinematics get expanded from a two dimensional scattering reaction to a three dimensional process where the reaction plane is determined by the plane spanned by the momentum vector of the projectile nucleus $\mathbf{p}_A$ and the scattered nucleon after the reaction \footnote{For (p,2p) reactions there is an ambiguity which of the scattered protons $\mathbf{p}_1$ or $\mathbf{p}_2$ origin from the projectile nucleus or the proton like target.}. With the momentum measurement of the two correlated outgoing nucleons and the momentum of the incoming projectile nucleus it is possible to retrieve the internal momentum of the knocked out nucleon perpendicular to the reaction plane $Q_{\perp i}$ via the formula derived in Ref.~\cite{chulkov2005quasi}
\begin{equation}\label{eq:chulkov}
Q_{\perp i} = sin(\theta_{1/2})\cdot sin(\varphi_{1/2} -\varphi_{2/1})\cdot\mathbf{p}_{2/1} \, .
\end{equation}
In case of (p,2p) reactions it is impossible to track back the origin of each nucleon - from the projectile nucleus or the proton-like target. Inferring from this there are two possible solutions for $Q_{\perp}$. The ambiguity can be resolved by incorporating the momentum of the fragment nucleus perpendicular to the reaction plane $Q_{\perp A-1}$. The fragment momentum vector should be of the same amount but point in opposite direction to $Q_{\perp i}$.\\
Up to this point, the scattering process has been described analogously to free elastic scattering in relativistic kinematics, with the knocked-out nucleon assumed to possess a non-negligible initial momentum inside the nucleus. However, it must be noted that the ejected nucleon is an off-shell particle, bound within the nuclear potential. Its virtual mass \( \mu_i \) in the c.m.s. of the nucleus is given by
\begin{equation}
\mu_i =  \sqrt{m_A^2 + m_{A-1}^2 - 2m_A\sqrt{m_{A-1}^2 + |\mathbf{p}_i|^2}} \, ,
\label{eq:virtual_mass}
\end{equation}
where \( m_A \) and \( m_{A-1} \) are the rest masses of the initial nucleus and the residual fragment after the quasi-free scattering, respectively, and \( \vec{p}_{i} \) denotes the momentum of the inner nucleon  in the c.m.s. of the nucleus, see also Eq.~\ref{eq:miss_mom}. From Eq.~\ref{eq:virtual_mass} follows
\begin{equation}
0 < \mu_i  < m_n \, ,
\end{equation}
where $m_n$ is the mass of the appropriate free nucleon.
To knock the bound nucleon out of the nucleus the separation energy has to be overcome which can be observed in the reduced opening angle of the two outgoing correlated nucleons.

\subsubsection{Application Fields of QFS Reactions}
Over several decades of experimental and theoretical research, Quasi-Free Scattering (QFS) reactions have been firmly established as a powerful and direct tool for probing the microscopic structure of atomic nuclei. These reactions provide critical insights into nuclear correlations, single-particle properties, and the momentum distributions of nucleons within the nucleus.\newline
The versatility of QFS extends across various applications, including the study of short-range nucleon-nucleon interactions, exotic nuclear states, and the modification of nucleon properties in nuclear matter. Advances in experimental facilities and state-of-the-art detector systems, have significantly improved the precision and scope of QFS measurements. These developments have enabled in-depth investigations of nuclear dynamics, the structure of unstable isotopes, and fundamental aspects of quantum many-body systems, contributing to a deeper understanding of nuclear and sub-nuclear phenomena.\newline
This subsection will point out the most exciting and promising application fields of QFS reactions with focus on the applicability in the R3B experimental setup. A detailed review can be found in Ref.~\cite{panin2021quasi}.
\begin{description}
\item[Single-Particle Spectroscopic Strength]As already mentioned in Section~\ref{sec:kin_qfs}, in many experiments a reduction in the spectroscopic strength of about 35\% with respect to the independent particle model (IPM) and shell model predictions was observed. In one-nucleon removal reactions -- experiments with isotope beams impinging on a composite nuclear target - the extraction of the missing spectroscopic strength is challenging as the kinematical pattern is highly complex. In contrast, the QFS reactions mechanism in inverse kinematics retains a clear kinematical signature which makes it a valuable tool to study the quenching of the spectroscopic strength which originates from residual correlations between bound nucleons inside the nucleus. \newline
Several experiments were carried out at the GSI Facility to study the reduction factor of the spectroscopic strength  and its evolution over a broad range in isotopic chains, in light nuclei such as for oxygen shown in figure \ref{fig:red_factor}. With the commissioning of the SIS100 and SuperFRS at GSI it will be achievable to study (p,2p) reactions with very short lived nuclei also in heavier systems at reasonable intensities which will presumably draw even more attention to QFS reaction approach.

\item[Gamma Spectroscopy]Alongside to the study of the single-particle spectroscopic strengths QFS reactions provide valuable information of the shell structure and deformation of the fragment (A-1) via gamma spectroscopy. QFS reactions with nucleons in inner shells result in bound (or unbound) excited states of the (A-1) fragment. The excited fragment promptly decays to its ground state either through the emission of a Doppler-shifted \(\gamma\)-ray, or -- if the excitation energy exceeds the particle separation threshold -- via the emission of a proton or neutron.\newline
A big advantage of the (p,2p) reaction experiments is the ability to make precise vertex reconstruction by accurate measurement of the two outgoing correlated protons. This is of particular importance for extended targets and determines the emission point within the target, enabling an accurate calculation of the fragment's velocity and therefore a precise Doppler correction.\newline
From gamma spectroscopy the excitation energy E(2+), often the first excited state in even-even nuclei, can be observed which is a fundamental observable of nuclear structure, providing key insights into the shell configuration and deformation of the nucleus~\cite{panin2021quasi}. Especially when going towards exotic neutron rich isotopes the study of the gamma emission lines can strongly contribute to the understanding of nuclear shell evolution.
\item[QFS to probe inner clustering and halo formation]Clusterization inside nuclei is a phenomenon widely observed in experiments with large implications on astrophysics processes such as the synthesis of carbon  inside stars via triple $\alpha$ clusterization~\cite{hjorth2011carbon}, $\alpha$-decay, or the evolution of core collapse supernovae~\cite{sumiyoshi2008appearance}, just to mention some. Its presence has also to be implemented in the state of the art equation of state calculations.\newline
Single-particle cluster states can be directly accessed via QFS (p,p$\alpha$) reactions. In the low energy regime of 100 AMeV the $^{12}\text{C}(p, p\alpha)^{8}\text{Be}$ reaction, see Ref.~\cite{mabiala2009analyzing}, as benchmark study approved the reaction mechanism and up to a scaling factor the measured cross sections aligned exceptionally well to the free p–$\alpha$ scattering measurements.\newline
Moreover the QFS mechanism allows to access light nuclei going towards the neutron drip line via (p,pn) reaction. These light nuclei are mostly weakly bound with an extended low density neutron-matter distribution forming a so-called \textit{nuclear-halo}.\newline
The most prominent candidate for such is $^{11}Li$, often called \textit{Borromean three-body system}, consisting of two neutrons interacting with a core ($^9Li$) via weak, short-range interactions~\cite{johannsen199011li}. First evidence for the correlation  between the two neutron was strongly pushed by experimental studies at GSI, see Ref.~\cite{simon1999direct}, and theoretical work by Bertulani et. al.~\cite{bertulani2007geometry}.\newline
The latest experiment with the focus on the study of multi-neutron correlations in drip-line nuclei was carried out at R3B in 2022 (experiment S509) probing broad isotopic chains of Li, Be, B, C and N via the QFS mechanism, and first results are expected to be published in the near future.
\item[Short Range Correlations(SRC)]Short range correlations refer to elementary nucleon-nucleon interactions within a nucleus and occur over very short distances, typically on the order of $1-2$ fm.  These correlations are characterized by pairs of nucleons with high relative momentum ($> k_F$, Fermi momentum) and low total momentum. These NN-interactions are treated as good explanatory candidate for observed deviations from mean field approximation. Many phenomena, such as the above discussed reduction in the spectroscopic strength and the EMC effect~\cite{xing2023electromagnetic}, the observation that  the cross section for deep inelastic scattering from an atomic nucleus is different from that of the same number of free protons and neutrons, are associated to the short range correlations inside the nucleus.\newline
From isotopic chain studies~\cite{clas2018probing} it has been shown that there is an indication of SRC dependency on isospin, see Fig.~\ref{fig:src_pairs}, where SRC are predominately preferred by p-n pairs than by nn or pp pairs. This again can have significant impact for asymmetric nuclei and imply a stronger quenching of the spectroscopic strength for proton single particle states below the Fermi momentum for neutron rich matter.\newline  
Since pioneering experiments were made at JLab and Brookhaven via $(e,e'p)$ and $(e,e'n)$ reactions several  experimental campaigns were carried out at GSI with the R3B setup probing the QFS reactions via the strong nuclear force instead of the Coulomb interaction. Also in 2022 the S522 experiment was performed to exploit for the first time the use of short-lived nuclei scattering off a proton probe in inverse kinematics at R3B, followed by the S091 experiment in 2024 with the focus on probing NN-correlations in atomic nuclei via $(p,pd)$ QFS reactions~\cite{paschalis2020nucleon}.

\item[Fission via QFS]Fission induced via quasifree scattering (QFS) provides a powerful method to extract fission barriers on an event-by-event basis. The fundamental concept involves the knockout of a proton or neutron from an incoming exotic ion beam. By measuring the energy  and the angular distribution of the correlated emitted nucleons, it becomes possible to probe the excitation energy transferred to the fissioning system. In cases where deeply bound nucleons are knocked out, the resulting daughter nucleus either evaporates one or multiple neutrons or populates an excited state, followed by the fission process. The R3B setup is particularly well-suited for such investigations, as it enables full kinematic reconstruction of the reaction products and hence to pin down the complete reaction mechanism. This approach allows for the detailed exploration of the potential-energy surface and the fission dynamics across a broad range of fissility and excitation energies, taking advantage of relativistic radioactive beams.\newline
The pilot experiment S455 was conducted in 2021 at R3B using a stable $^{238}$U beam incident on a liquid hydrogen target. The data collected from this experiment provide simultaneous information on several key fission observables, which can be used to constrain theoretical calculations. These calculations aim to describe both the static and dynamic properties of the fission process and enable comparisons with predictions from various theoretical frameworks, including phenomenological approaches, macroscopic-microscopic models, and fully microscopic statistical or time-dependent Hartree-Fock calculations.\newline
\end{description}
